
### Round 224. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Tax-credit "re-amortization" loans: the quoted monthly payment is a teaser that jumps when the customer doesn't prepay the assumed credit
- Attack: Lender product in the policy table is a 25-year solar loan built around a voluntary prepayment of ~30% at month 18. The calculator quotes the pre-re-amortization payment ($189). READ_BACK speaks "financing terms" from table keys: rate, term, $189/month. Customer, a retiree with no tax liability (and no 25D credit at all for 2026 installs), never prepays. At month 19 the payment re-amortizes to ~$270. The commit was gated on a number that was never the real payment. CFPB's 2024 solar financing spotlight flags exactly this structure: payments rise sharply if the assumed tax-credit prepayment isn't made. The design's read-back lists what to say, not that the spoken payment must be the payment the customer will actually owe. Lints check Reg Z trigger terms, not payment-shock structure. Source: files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_solar-financing-issue-spotlight_2024-08.pdf
- Defense: The payment spoken at READ_BACK isn't free calculator output. R154 makes "the lender program's fee schedule ... a versioned policy-table input", and the financed total is "the lender contract price". R32 requires a "representative fully-disclosed example from versioned lender rate sheet". R85 requires READ_BACK to include "financing terms (Reg Z)". The tax credit can't prop up the payment either: R155 types incentive rows, and "capped and reservation rows can never reduce the quoted or read-back price". An expired 25D row would be refused as outside its effective_from/until window (R140). R150's suitability row compares the financed payment with verified savings. The gap is that no rule says which payment in a multi-step schedule gets spoken.
- Verdict: R154, R32 and R85 make the spoken financing terms come from the lender rate sheet and require "financing terms (Reg Z)" in READ_BACK, but nothing says which payment in a multi-step schedule gets spoken. R157's year-1/final-year rule covers escalator leases only, and R150's affordability check doesn't say which step it tests. The Designer concedes this gap, and the defense rests on new hardening. R155/R140 stop an expired 25D row from cutting the price, but a re-amortizing loan still quotes the pre-step payment.
- Fix: Every lender product row carries its full Reg Z payment schedule. Where a product assumes a voluntary prepayment or re-amortizes, READ_BACK speaks every scheduled step ("<pay_1> until month <n>, then <pay_2>") with the no-prepayment payment as the payment owed, and the R150 affordability row is tested against the highest step. Products whose payment depends on a tax credit the customer can't be shown to receive are excluded from on-call close.

### Round 225. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Financing terms read back before the lender's credit decision, so the commit is gated on an assumed credit tier
- Attack: The lender runs the application after the call (the design keeps underwriting with the lender). To read back "financing terms" on the call, the calculator has to assume a tier, in practice the best one ("as low as"). Customer says "yes" to $162/month at 3.99%. The lender approves them at tier C, 9.99%, $231/month, in the e-sign envelope. Either the read-back carried hypothetical terms, or the agent pulls credit mid-call, which is a hard inquiry on a cold call. The COMMIT gate certifies agreement to read-back values. When the lender owns the decision, those values can't be lender_verified at commit time. "Same menu for all" makes this worse: everyone hears the top-tier price, and lower-credit buyers, who are disproportionately protected groups, get the surprise.
- Defense: The design never lets an assumed tier reach a commit. Credit facts are system-owned: "credit band slot accepted only with verified (lender prequal) provenance, never from speech" (R90), and provenance includes lender_verified. No Needle tool sets a credit fact (R5 writer allowlists). The lint bans requesting credit consent by voice and the word "pre-qualified" (R89, R96), so no mid-call hard pull happens. If the lender's decision changes the terms, R60 applies: "conditional approval at new amount invalidates quote hash". The R134 outbox monitor refuses CONTRACT_SENT unless READ_BACK played for the same quote hash. Same menu for all (R46) means the same products are offered, not the same rate. Matched-pair tests check path divergence.
- Verdict: The design already blocks an assumed tier. Credit band is accepted only with lender-prequal provenance and never from speech (R90). The calculator takes lender prequal as an input (R79), and missing inputs mean no figure (R29). The lints ban voice credit consent and "pre-qualified" (R89/R96). If the decision later moves the terms, R60, R23 and R154(3) invalidate the quote hash and force a new read-back, so a mismatched envelope can't go out.

### Round 226. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Interconnecting forces a rate-plan switch, so savings are computed against a tariff the customer won't be on after install
- Attack: Verified usage comes from a California customer on a tiered or legacy TOU plan. The calculator prices avoided cost at that plan's rates. Under the Net Billing Tariff, solar customers must take service on an eligible TOU rate, and utilities put them on high-differential electrification rates with low midday value and an expensive 4-9pm peak. The savings fragment's baseline (current plan) and post-solar (new mandatory plan) are different tariffs. The quoted savings compare apples to a rate the customer will lose. The design says "savings only from verified usage". Usage is verified, but the rate it is multiplied by is chosen by the utility because of the sale itself. That's not a forecast (A206). It's a known, mandatory, present-day change the calculator ignores. Source: CPUC NBT decision D.22-12-056; cpuc.ca.gov/nem
- Defense: R157 already rejects a single-tariff shortcut: "The calculator must use the customer's exact tariff schedule (e.g. NBT hourly export) on verified interval data, else no figure." A tariff-filing monitor expires rows. R212 puts "a tariff_class key on every calculator row, read from the utility authorization API". Under NBT the eligible post-solar rate is a published, present-day rule, so it can be a versioned policy-table row like any other tariff. The attack is right that "exact tariff" should name both sides of the comparison, and the files don't say so.
- Verdict: R157's "customer's exact tariff schedule (e.g. NBT hourly export)" values exports under NBT. It still reads as the customer's current import schedule, but CPUC's NBT decision requires solar customers to take service on an eligible high-differential/electrification TOU rate. The design never names a separate post-solar tariff, and the Designer admits this. So the baseline and the post-solar bill can be priced on a tariff the customer will lose.
- Fix: The calculator computes two bills on verified interval data: the baseline on the current schedule, and the post-solar bill on a policy-table interconnection_tariff row (the mandated rate per utility and program). Savings = baseline minus post-solar. READ_BACK must state the rate change. If several eligible rates are possible, use the least favorable one or give no figure.

### Round 227. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Community Choice Aggregator customers: generation rates and export credits are set by the CCA, not the utility tariff the calculator uses
- Attack: Customer in Marin or San Diego gets generation service from a CCA (MCE, SDCP) and delivery from the IOU. The policy table holds IOU tariffs keyed by utility territory. The calculator values offset and exports at IOU generation rates and IOU NEM rules. The CCA has different generation rates, its own export-credit adders and an annual cash-out policy, and can change them by board vote without CPUC rate cases. Utility territory isn't the rate-setter for about a quarter of California load. Nothing in the slot store records the generation provider, and verified usage (kWh) doesn't reveal it. Every savings number for CCA customers is computed against the wrong tariff while being "certified" as calculator_output with a substantiation id.
- Defense: Savings figures already need the customer's exact tariff on verified data, else no figure (R157). The only writer for verified_usage is the utility data API or the deterministic bill extractor (R156). Bills and Green Button data for bundled-delivery CCA customers identify the generation provider and its charges. So the provider can be read from those sources and doesn't have to be guessed from territory. The real gap is that no slot stores the provider, so the calculator can default to IOU generation rates.
- Verdict: Nothing in the design records the generation provider. Calculator rows are keyed by utility and tariff_class (R212), so a CCA customer's generation rate, export adders and cash-out policy can silently default to IOU rows. "Exact tariff, else no figure" (R157) can't fire when the system doesn't know a different rate-setter exists. The Designer concedes the missing slot.
- Fix: Add a generation_provider slot, written only by the utility API or bill extractor. Key calculator rows by (delivery utility, generation provider, schedule), and add CCA rate/export/cash-out rows watched by the tariff-filing monitor. An unknown provider or missing row means no figure and a written proposal.

### Round 228. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Discount-rate households (CARE/FERA, medical baseline) get savings computed at full price, overstating benefit for the poorest customers
- Attack: Customer is on CARE, which is roughly a 30-35% discount on the electric bill, plus a lower income-graduated fixed charge. Verified usage gives kWh, and the calculator multiplies it by the standard residential rate. It reads back "$140/month savings" against a bill that really costs them about $90 at discounted rates. The loan payment is real dollars, and the "savings" are against prices the customer never paid. Medical-baseline customers get the same distortion. The rate discount isn't a usage fact, so "verified usage" doesn't fix it. The error falls entirely on low-income and medically vulnerable households, the group that A199's affordability gap and the fair-lending matched-pair tests are meant to protect. A matched-pair test passes because both pairs get the same wrong rate.
- Defense: "Savings only from verified usage" never meant usage alone. R157 requires the customer's exact tariff schedule, else no figure. R212 reads tariff_class from the utility authorization API. CARE, FERA and medical baseline are billing attributes in the same utility data, not things the caller has to tell us. R150 limits the downside: the financed payment must be ≤ the calculator-verified savings band, so an inflated savings number is exactly what that check is meant to catch. A matched-pair test passing on a shared wrong rate is a real blind spot, and I add a check that doesn't depend on pairs.
- Verdict: CARE/FERA and medical baseline are part of the customer's billed tariff schedule. At PG&E, for example, CARE customers are on distinct schedules, and the utility API reports them. R157 requires that exact schedule, else no figure, and R156 restricts the data source to the utility API or bill extractor. A calculator that multiplies by the standard residential rate already breaks an existing rule. Matched-pair tests were never the control for calculation correctness.

### Round 229. Winner: Designer
- Topic: The savings baseline includes charges solar can't offset (fixed charges, minimum bills, non-bypassable charges)
- Attack: Customer's verified bill is $210. The calculator computes offset as 100% of annual kWh, so the fragment says "your bill drops to near zero". In California, the income-graduated fixed charge adopted May 2024 (about $24/month for most customers) plus non-bypassable charges on all imports keep being billed regardless of production. Other utilities have minimum bills or grid-access charges. Year one, the customer pays the loan plus about $40-60/month in electric bills they were told would disappear. The design checks where each number comes from, not what it means. A "savings" fact assembled as bill minus production value is unsubstantiated if parts of the bill aren't volumetric. FTC s5 and the TSR forbid misrepresenting material costs. Sources: utilitydive.com/news/california-puc-approves-income-based-fixed-charge/715748/; sce.com fixed-charge FAQ
- Defense: The scenario's key line, "your bill drops to near zero", is already banned: R18's build-time banned-claims lint covers "zero bill", and the runtime lint runs on rendered replies (R29). R157 requires the exact tariff schedule, and a tariff schedule includes its fixed, minimum and non-bypassable components. R72 requires savings wording to carry calculator_output with a substantiation id, and R161's principle (a substantiation id must be backed by something real) applies here as well. Still, the files don't say outright that the post-solar bill includes non-volumetric charges.
- Verdict: The scenario's key line, "near zero bill", is already a banned claim (R18) that the runtime lint checks on rendered replies (R29). A calculator bound to the exact tariff schedule on interval data (R157) must bill fixed and non-bypassable components, which carry through both bills and can't be offset away. The design doesn't spell out the post-solar bill composition, but the existing rule already forbids the "100% offset of the bill" shortcut the attack relies on.

### Round 230. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Production is self-reported: savings need modeled kWh from roof azimuth, tilt and shading that a phone call can't verify
- Attack: QUALIFY asks "Any shade on your roof?" and the customer says "Not really". The calculator assumes standard south-facing production for the size. The roof is actually east-west split, 14° tilt, with a neighbor's oak shading it after 2pm. Real production is 30% below the estimate, so the savings and payback in the read-back are off by a third, even though usage was verified to the kWh. The design pins verified provenance on usage only. Production, the other half of every savings number, gets no slot provenance class (no "verified_design"). So a self-reported spoken "not really" feeds a certified calculator_output. Garbage in, certified fact out.
- Defense: Production isn't taken from speech. R79: "calculator inputs include roof/shade data lookup". R211(3) cross-checks shade "against aerial" data. R61: savings and sizing figures come "never from spoken estimates". R71 makes shade a qualification slot, so "not really" only routes suitability and never feeds a figure. R159 schedules the site survey inside the cancellation window and makes the contract "contingent on a survey-confirmed price", with re-disclosure, fresh e-sign and a no-penalty exit on any change. What's missing is a named provenance class for production.
- Verdict: Production doesn't come from speech. R79 puts the roof/shade data lookup into calculator inputs, R211(3) cross-checks shade against aerial data, and R61 forbids savings or sizing from spoken estimates. Shade is only a qualification slot that routes suitability (R71). R159 makes the contract contingent on a survey-confirmed price, with re-disclosure and a no-penalty exit. A spoken "not really" can't become a certified production figure.

### Round 231. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Lifetime savings and payback omit known future costs: inverter replacement, remove-and-reinstall for a reroof, insurance and property tax
- Attack: The policy table's "25-year savings" sums avoided bills minus loan payments. String inverters are typically warranted for 10-12 years, so a replacement around year 12 costs about $2-4k. QUALIFY recorded a 15-year-old roof that passed suitability, but it will need reroofing during the term, which means about $3-6k for detach-and-reset. Homeowner's insurance riders and, in some states, property tax on added value also go unmentioned. The read-back states the lifetime number as certified fact. Every cost the system has on record (roof age, equipment type, term) predicts these expenses, and the deterministic calculator ignores them. A substantiation id proves the formula ran, not that the claim is complete. An omitted material cost is deceptive under FTC s5 even when each number shown is true.
- Defense: Several of these costs are already gated. The roof case: "roof age ... qualification slots" feed the suitability table and route to ROOF_FIRST (R71). That is the row for a roof that won't outlast the term. Future-value figures must carry R157's "estimate, assumes X, not guaranteed" qualifier, paired with a zero-escalation case. R161 says who backs each warranty, and R83 requires material limitations in READ_BACK. But a lifetime savings sum that leaves out predictable costs is a completeness gap, and provenance checks can't catch it.
- Verdict: The design lets multi-year projections be spoken with R157's "estimate, assumes X" qualifier, but no rule requires a lifetime or payback figure to subtract predictable costs the system already knows about, such as inverter replacement within the term or detach-and-reset for a roof that passed suitability but won't outlast the term. A qualifier on assumptions doesn't cure a material omission. The Designer calls it a completeness gap and relies on new bans and expected-cost rows.
- Fix: Ban lifetime-savings and payback keys from phone fragments: only year-one savings on the call, multi-year figures only in the written proposal. Any multi-year figure must subtract policy-table expected_cost rows keyed on equipment type versus term (inverter replacement), remaining roof life versus term (detach-and-reset) and jurisdiction (insurance and property tax). A suitability row sends any roof whose remaining life is shorter than the term to ROOF_FIRST.

### Round 232. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Quote validity across WAITING_FOR_SIGNATURE: the price table changes between the spoken read-back and a signature days later
- Attack: Tuesday: READ_BACK speaks $3.10/W, policy table v41, and the envelope is sent. Wednesday: equipment cost update ships v42 at $3.35/W, and certificates citing v41 expire. Saturday: customer signs. Which price is the contract? If the envelope froze v41, the company sells at a retired price with an expired certificate. If it regenerates, the signed price differs from the one the commit gate verified. Either way, there's a re-send question the graph never models. The design tracks status "only from vendor webhooks" and expires certificates when rows change. It has no quote-expiry date in the read-back, no binding of envelope to table version, and no re-read-back path when they diverge. The verbal-commit-to-paper link breaks exactly at a version bump.
- Defense: The contract is bound to what was read back, not to the live table. R23 says derived values are "computed from versioned inputs" and "read-back bound to config hash". R49 says accept_offer "commits only the quote hash read back". R134's outbox monitor refuses CONTRACT_SENT unless READ_BACK played for the same quote hash, so the envelope carries v41. Certificate expiry (R140, "certificates expire when a cited policy row changes") stops future calls from speaking retired rows. It doesn't void offers already made. Any price change after the read-back follows R159: new quote hash, written re-disclosure, fresh e-sign, no-penalty exit. What's missing is an explicit validity period.
- Verdict: The envelope is bound to the read-back. R49 commits only the quote hash read back, R23 ties the read-back to a config hash over versioned inputs, and the R134 outbox monitor refuses CONTRACT_SENT without READ_BACK for that same hash. So the envelope carries v41, and a later table bump doesn't rewrite it. Certificate expiry stops new calls from speaking retired rows. It isn't contract validity. Any later price change goes through R159's re-disclosure path.

### Round 233. Winner: Designer
- Topic: The verified usage history isn't the household's future load: new owners, prior occupants, planned EVs and heat pumps
- Attack: Customer bought the house 7 months ago. The utility's 12-month history is half the previous owner's (two people, no AC) and half theirs (five people, AC). Or the customer mentions "we're getting an EV next month". The calculator sizes and prices savings on the verified 12 months anyway, because only verified_usage may feed savings and the EV remark is ungrounded speech. The system ends up undersized, and the savings fragment is wrong in either direction. "Verified" means the utility confirms past kWh, not that it represents this household's next 25 years. The design forbids using spoken load changes (no provenance), so it must either quote known-wrong savings or none. Sizing rules (often 100-120% of historical usage) lock the error in at interconnection.
- Defense: The design picked the honest failure mode on purpose. Figures come from verified history (R156, R61). Sizing is "capped to the authorized meter's verified usage" (R210), so the system can't be oversized on a spoken EV plan. A smaller system is a conservative error. The claim stays true: it's savings against the household's verified past usage. The EV remark isn't lost either: customer-mentioned facts become provenance slots in the discourse register (R4), and R5's claim_prior_promise logs customer_asserted. R157's assumption-provenance type is the lawful way to talk about future load. What's missing is detecting that part of the history belongs to someone else.
- Verdict: The design deliberately chooses the conservative, truthful path. Figures come only from verified usage (R61/R156), sizing is capped to verified meter usage (R210), and spoken future loads can't upsize anything. They are only recorded as customer-mentioned provenance slots (R4), and any projection must use R157's assumption-typed qualifier. The mixed-occupant scenario is also weak, because account-holder usage authorization (R156(2)) normally returns only the current account holder's data, not the prior occupant's.
