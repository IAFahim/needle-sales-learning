
### Round 154. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Lender dealer fees buried in the "total cost" the policy table and calculator quote, so the cash and financed prices silently diverge
- Attack: Price table lists a system at $30,000. Customer picks the 25-year loan. The lender's program charges the installer a 25% dealer fee, so the installer's financed contract price is $40,000 while the quote stays at $30,000. Calculator reads the price row, not the lender's fee schedule. READ_BACK recites the table's "total cost" and an APR that passes the Reg Z lint. The contract the customer signs by SMS shows the $40,000 price. The cash buyer next door would have paid $30,000. Policy rows are seller-side facts; dealer fees are set by the lender and per program, outside the table. "Same menu for all" doesn't stop financed buyers paying a hidden markup. CFPB's 2024 solar spotlight found dealer fees often above 30%, hidden in the price and leaving a low-looking APR. The trigger-term lint can't see this. https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_solar-financing-issue-spotlight_2024-08.pdf
- Defense: The design already treats the dealer fee as part of financing, not price: R32 puts "dealer fee in finance charge" and requires a "representative fully-disclosed example from versioned lender rate sheet". The R85 READ_BACK must state total cost and financing terms (Reg Z), and the R23 read-back is "bound to config hash", so a financed quote with a different amount is a different quote. R60 covers the lender side: "conditional approval at new amount invalidates quote hash". The R46 product-policy table ("all options to all") plus matched-pair tests make a financed-vs-cash divergence measurable. The real gap is that nothing yet forces the e-sign contract amount to equal the amount read back.
- Verdict: The CFPB scenario rests on a dealer fee hidden in the price so the APR looks low. R32 in design.md already puts the dealer fee in the finance charge and takes the representative example from a versioned lender rate sheet. R23 and R60 bind consent to the config/quote hash, and a new lender amount invalidates it. The Designer admits one residual gap: no invariant yet forces the e-sign contract amount to equal the read-back amount. That is hardening on a mechanism that already targets the attack.

### Round 155. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Net price quoted after a capped, first-come incentive that is exhausted or needs a reservation by the time of install
- Attack: Policy table row: "state battery rebate, $X per kWh," substantiated by the program's published step. QUOTE fragment presents the net price after the rebate. The customer signs on that net figure. During the cancellation-window wait, the program's funding step fills, or the application is rejected because the installer had to reserve before signing. The rebate drops to the next lower step or zero. The contract obligates the gross price. Certificates only expire when a cited row changes, and nobody edited the row. The substantiation id proves the rebate existed, not that this customer will get it. Capped programs are queue states held outside the bundle. The design deliberately delays install, which puts customers at the back of the queue. A deterministic, certified net-price claim becomes a misrepresentation (FTC s5) with no runtime check on reservation status.
- Defense: The design never lets an unverifiable external fact gate a number. R29's calculator gives "insufficient-inputs -> no figure", R78 "bans ... unverifiable external-fact fragments", and R18's banned-claims lint (e.g. "guaranteed credits") already covers promising money the seller can't guarantee. Facts rows have "effective dates" and certificates "expire when a cited policy row changes". The R85 READ_BACK must state "total cost", meaning the contract obligation, not a net figure. The attack is right that a capped, first-come rebate is queue state. It shouldn't be modelled as a static row, and the delay from the cancellation window does make that worse.
- Verdict: Nothing in the design stops a policy-table rebate row from reducing the spoken or read-back price. A substantiation id proves the program exists, not that this customer gets the money. R78's "unverifiable external fact" lint is about urgency and weather pretexts, not queue-state incentives. Certificates expire only on row edits, so a filled funding step leaves a certified net-price claim that is now false, made worse by the install delay the design itself imposes.
- Fix: Type incentive rows (guaranteed / capped / reservation_required). Capped and reservation rows can never reduce the quoted or read-back price. READ_BACK states the gross contract price and the incentive only as "if approved, not guaranteed". A net price may be spoken only with a program_reserved provenance slot set by a program or installer API event. A daily program-status feed expires capped rows. The contract carries an incentive-failure no-penalty cancel clause, and the reservation is filed inside the cancellation window.

### Round 156. Winner: Designer
- Topic: "Verified usage" can't be obtained on a cold call: utility interval data requires the account holder's separate authorization
- Attack: QUOTE needs a verified_usage slot. Utilities release interval data only through an authorization flow (utility login, Green Button Connect, signed release) by the account holder. On the call, the account is in a tenant's or late spouse's name, or the customer won't hand over utility credentials. Writer allowlists bar spoken usage from becoming verified_usage. Every call ends with no savings figure, or the team adds a "spoken bill amount" writer to rescue conversion, which is exactly the provenance the design forbids. The design assumes a data feed it never specifies. Utility data access is consent-gated by state utility privacy rules and depends on the account holder, not the record owner the IDENTITY step verified. The outcomes are either no savings pitch, and unit economics collapse, or an allowlist exception that reopens customer-asserted numbers into savings claims.
- Defense: The attack describes the designed behavior, not a failure. R61 says savings figures come "only from verified bill or usage data, never spoken estimates". R72 says "spoken amounts only fill coarse routing bands", and the writer allowlists plus "slot-source allowlist enforced at build time" make a "spoken bill amount" writer a failed build. A team can't quietly add one. R29 handles missing data: "insufficient-inputs -> no figure, offer survey". The call still covers product, price, warranties and financing without a savings claim, and R28 tracks "time-on-fallback ... as a throughput metric", so the economics are visible. Unit economics are never a reason to reopen provenance. What's missing is the path by which verified usage legitimately arrives.
- Verdict: The existing design fails safe exactly as intended. R61 allows savings only from verified bill or usage data, R72 enforces slot-source allowlists at build time (so a "spoken bill" writer fails the build), and R29 gives "insufficient inputs -> no figure, offer survey". R28 already tracks time-on-fallback as a throughput metric. Weaker conversion is a cost, not a safety or compliance break, and the design cannot be quietly loosened.

### Round 157. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Savings depend on a future utility tariff and rate-escalation assumption, not on usage; NEM changes make the certified savings fragment a forecast stated as fact
- Attack: Calculator takes verified 12-month usage times a policy-table "utility escalation 4%/yr" and the current export rate. A California customer is on the Net Billing Tariff, where export credit is far below retail and varies by hour. The calculator's single export rate overstates savings. Next year, the utility restructures fixed charges and the escalation row is never touched. Customers sign 25-year escalator leases priced against projected rates that don't arrive. "Savings only from verified usage" checks inputs, not the model. Export value and future rates are assumptions, and a policy-table key gives them a substantiation id they don't deserve. NEM 3.0 cut average export compensation about 75%, and a court upheld it in March 2026. https://www.pv-tech.org/new-california-net-metering-proposal-would-slash-export-rates-critics-warn ; https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/03/10/california-court-upholds-nem-3-0-dealing-blow-to-rooftop-solar
- Defense: The calculator isn't a single-number guess. R29 specifies "calculator with versioned tariff tables", and R79 makes calculator inputs explicit. R18 bans "zero bill" and "guaranteed credits", and the facts table carries "effective dates". So a tariff change is a row change, and certificates "expire when a cited policy row changes". The attack does land on two points: a flat export rate can't represent Net Billing Tariff hourly credits, and an escalation percentage with a substantiation id dresses up an assumption as a fact. Projections need a different provenance and different wording than verified facts.
- Verdict: R29's "versioned tariff tables" and R72's calculator_output provenance check where the inputs came from, not whether the model is right. Nothing requires hourly Net Billing Tariff export values, and nothing marks rate-escalation or future-tariff rows as assumptions. A single export rate or a 4%/yr escalator carrying a substantiation id is a forecast stated as fact. The Designer concedes both points.
- Fix: Add an "assumption" provenance type for escalation and future-rate rows. Any fragment filled from one must carry a lint-enforced "estimate, assumes X, not guaranteed" qualifier. The default savings figure uses today's rates only, and any projection is paired with a zero-escalation case. The calculator must use the customer's exact tariff schedule (e.g. NBT hourly export) on verified interval data, else no figure. A tariff-filing monitor expires rows. Escalator-lease READ_BACK states the year-1 and final-year payments.

### Round 158. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Interconnection and permission-to-operate gap: loan payments and the "installed" success label arrive before the utility lets the system run
- Attack: Contract signed, cancellation window waited out, panels installed. The utility's interconnection review requires a transformer or service upgrade, or the queue runs months. Loan payments start on the installation milestone. The customer pays the loan plus a full utility bill with a dead system. CRM marks "installed, not rescinded, no complaint yet." The success label joins, and this call trains and certifies as a win. A frustrated customer complains to the AG in month five, after the label is frozen. The design's success definition ends at install, not PTO or energization, and READ_BACK never discloses utility-side upgrade costs, approval risk or payment start timing. Interconnection is a third-party decision with costs the price table can't hold. The release gate is measured on labels that count non-operating systems as successes.
- Defense: R71 defines success as "installed AND not rescinded AND no complaint ... AND passes sampled suitability audit". R50 joins labels from install records, and R83 already requires "material-limitation disclosures" in every read-back. But the release gate isn't measured on those labels. R2 and R66 make it a "per-call harm bound measured only on real calls labeled by humans listening to audio". A label error affects training reward and metrics, not certification. The attack is right that success should mean energized, and that READ_BACK doesn't list interconnection risk or when payments start.
- Verdict: The claim that certification uses these labels is wrong: R2 and R66 gate release on audio-audited per-call harm. But the design's own success label (R71: installed, not rescinded, no complaint, which feeds training reward, the pass criterion and R50 branch metrics) counts systems that are installed but not operating. READ_BACK (R85) has no required disclosure of utility approval risk, upgrade cost or when payments start relative to PTO. An audio audit cannot see harm that happens months after the call.
- Fix: Redefine success as PTO granted plus N months of monitored production plus no complaint across the whole window, with labels provisional until then. Add policy-table rows required in READ_BACK and the contract: utility interconnection approval required, possible utility-side upgrade cost and who pays it, and the exact loan first-payment event. Make an install-to-PTO lag over threshold, and any "paying, not operating" state, Tier A harm signals per branch.

### Round 159. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Post-signature site survey change orders (main panel upgrade, reroof, trenching, structural) changing the price after the read-back the commit was gated on
- Attack: The phone quote has no site visit. After signing, the installer's surveyor finds a 100A panel, rafters needing reinforcement and a roof with five years left. They issue a change order: +$4,800 MPU, +$2,100 structural. The customer is told the system can't go in otherwise, and the cancellation window has passed. The lender re-papers the loan amount by e-sign. The AI's certified READ_BACK, the legal anchor of consent, no longer describes the deal. Every commit path passes READ_BACK, but the real price is set later by a subcontractor's survey the graph never sees. Suitability checks roof age and shade from customer answers, not electrical service size. Waiting out the cancellation window before install means change orders always land after cancellation rights have expired. That is the bait-and-switch pattern regulators target.
- Defense: The design already makes price changes after read-back void consent: R23 says "any change after read-back forces a new read-back", R49 says "accept_offer commits only the quote hash read back", and R60 says "conditional approval at new amount invalidates quote hash". R71's ROOF_FIRST ("inspection before any quote") handles roof age, and the original close definition schedules the "site survey ... as the next step". A change order is therefore a new quote that the design already refuses to treat as consented. What's missing is enforcing that downstream of the call, and the timing relative to the cancellation window.
- Verdict: R23, R49 and R60 bind consent to the hash read back on the call, but nothing in the design governs an installer's post-signature change order. Suitability (R71) has no electrical-service slot. Because install waits out the cancellation window (R17), every change order lands after cancellation rights expire. The Designer concedes that enforcement downstream of the call and the timing are missing.
- Fix: Schedule the site survey inside the cancellation window and make the contract contingent on a survey-confirmed price. Every change order becomes a new quote hash with written re-disclosure, a fresh e-sign by all signers, a no-penalty exit and a restart of the cancellation window. Install is blocked until then. Add main-panel amperage and roof-structure slots. When they are unknown, READ_BACK must state "price subject to survey" with policy-table cost ranges. An unconsented price increase is Tier A harm.

### Round 160. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Equipment substitution after signing (tariffs, supply shortages, discontinued SKUs) making the named product and warranties in the read-back false
- Attack: READ_BACK names panel model P and inverter I with their manufacturers' warranties from the policy table. Between signing and install (delayed by the cancellation window), new tariffs or a supply gap make P unavailable. The installer subcontractor swaps in a different brand with a shorter product warranty and a different degradation curve. The customer isn't told until the crew arrives. The policy row and certificate still cite P. Contract, install and the AI's certified statement now disagree. Fragments certify words, not fulfilment. The product facts the commit was gated on are controlled by a downstream supply chain and a subcontractor. The design has no post-sale reconciliation that re-discloses or re-consents when the installed BOM differs from the read-back, so the deterministic "truthful claim" is true only on the day of the call.
- Defense: R82 requires READ_BACK to state the "explicit product type/size/price from quote object", and R23 binds consent to a "config hash". The named model and warranties are therefore part of what the customer agreed to, not loose wording. A different SKU is a different config, which the design already says can't ride on the old yes. R91 separates product_warranty_years from workmanship_warranty_years as distinct table keys. The attack correctly notes there's no reconciliation step between the signed config and the installed bill of materials.
- Verdict: R82 and R23 make the named product part of the consented config, but no step compares what is installed to what was signed. The design controls install release (an outbox effect that waits for the cancellation window) yet never gates it on a bill-of-materials match. So the certified read-back can be silently falsified by substitution, which the Designer admits.
- Fix: Gate the install effect on WAITING_FOR_BOM_MATCH, comparing the installer's planned SKUs (then installed serials) to the signed config hash. On a mismatch, block install and require a written re-disclosure of the new product, warranty and degradation terms plus a fresh e-sign with a no-penalty cancel. Add a supply-status field to product rows that expires discontinued or tariff-hit SKUs. Count an un-reconsented substitution as Tier A harm.

### Round 161. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Installer or partner insolvency leaving the named workmanship and production warranties with no one behind them
- Attack: The policy table substantiates "25-year workmanship warranty" with the installer partner's contract. The partner, paid by the lender at install milestones, goes bankrupt mid-season. Customers signed last month have loans funded and roofs half-done. Earlier customers hold warranties nobody honors. The certified fragment keeps saying "25-year warranty" because the row's text didn't change. Only the counterparty died. Substantiation ids point at documents, not at a solvent obligor. Milestone disbursement pays dealers before completion. SunPower's August 2024 Chapter 11 and other installer collapses left homeowners with unfinished work and disputed warranties. Certificates expire on row edits, not on counterparty credit events. https://pv-magazine.com/2024/08/07/sunpower-goes-bankrupt ; https://news.bloombergtax.com/california-brief/solar-panel-bankruptcies-leave-frustrated-homeowners-in-limbo
- Defense: Warranty claims already cite substantiation ids in one owned, versioned policy table (R91, R18), and certificates "expire when a cited policy row changes". The mechanism for pulling a claim exists. Lender decisions and disbursement stay with the lender ("lender runs application and decisions"). The attack shows the trigger is too narrow, though. Substantiation points at a document, not at a solvent obligor, and nothing watches counterparty health.
- Verdict: A warranty term in a signed contract is a true statement when made. ### Round 162. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Dealer or lead-partner fraud through the lead record: fabricated identity answers and partner-controlled phone numbers receiving the e-sign OTP
- Attack: A dealer partner paid per signed contract uploads leads. For a real elderly homeowner's address, it creates a record with an accomplice's mobile number and "challenge" answers it wrote. The AI calls the accomplice, the challenge-response "verifies" against the fraudster's own record, and the accomplice affirms the read-back. The e-sign SMS and OTP go to the same phone. The lender funds and a UCC/lien lands on the real owner's home. IDENTITY verifies against the lead record, which is partner-supplied, and SMS OTP proves only phone possession. Record-owner names come from public records, so nothing ties the signer's phone to the titleholder. Forged-signature and unauthorized installs are a documented solar fraud pattern. https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2024/08/14/what-happens-when-solar-is-installed-without-homeowners-permission ; https://www.npr.org/2024/08/14/1244330369/solar-rooftop-panels-environment-fraud-deception
- Defense: Several existing layers already work against this. R74 needs a "PEWC artifact from that subscriber", and R73 checks the Reassigned Numbers Database. R88 looks up record owners "on validated address" from public records, not the partner's lead, and the envelope goes to "all record owners + lender stipulated signers". The lender runs the application and its own identity checks. R15's vulnerability counter and R71's sampled suitability audit catch some elder cases. The attack is right that the challenge-response in R73 checks against a partner-supplied record, and that an SMS OTP proves only possession of a phone. Nothing ties the phone to the titleholder.
- Verdict: R73's challenge-response checks answers against the partner-supplied lead record, and R74's consent artifact can come from the same fraudulent partner. R22 sends the e-sign OTP to the dialed number. R88 sets the signer names from public records, but delivery and OTP still go to the fraudster's phone, so nothing ties the signer to the titleholder. The design leaves this open, as its own "lead-vendor consent audit" open item shows.
- Fix: Draw identity-challenge answers only from independent sources (public records, KYC vendor), never from partner lead fields. Run a pre-dial phone-to-record-owner match (carrier or identity graph); with no match, the call is lead-gen only. Require lender-grade identity verification (document plus selfie or KBA) for each record owner at e-sign. Mail a contract summary with cancel instructions to the property address of record and hold install for a set time after it. Track per-partner fraud metrics with auto-suspension, and use an independent consent-capture provider.

### Round 163. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Success labels from the CRM, controlled by installers and ops staff who are paid on them, gamed to certify bad branches
- Attack: Success = installed, not rescinded, no complaint. Install subcontractors and the ops team enter install dates and complaint tickets in the CRM, and their bonuses depend on low complaint and cancellation rates. They log angry calls as "service inquiry," offer small credits to drop complaints, and backdate installs. Suitability audits sample from what the CRM shows. A branch that closes vulnerable customers into change orders shows a clean success label, and the release gate certifies it. Joining labels from a CRM that interested parties write puts the training and certification signal in the hands of people with incentives to suppress harm. The design's integrity controls (hashes, replay, invariants) cover the call. None cover the label pipeline. Goodhart: the per-call harm bound becomes whatever the installer chose to record.
- Defense: The release gate doesn't read the CRM. R2 and R66 measure the "per-call harm bound measured only on real calls labeled by humans listening to audio ... on whole audited call traces". Gaming CRM fields can't certify a branch that the audio audit flags. R51 already says "labels never from self-reported outcomes". R71's "sampled suitability audit" is a separate check, and "unsuitable close = Tier A harm". The attack still hits training reward and the success metric, because R50 joins outcomes through a CRM that interested parties write. Those inputs need their own integrity controls.
- Verdict: The release gate is audio-based (R2/R66), so the "certifies it" claim is overstated. But post-call harm is invisible on audio. The only signals for it are the R71 success label, R50 cancellation-rate-per-branch tracking and the sampled suitability audit, and all of them flow through a CRM written by people paid on those numbers. R51's "no self-reported labels" targets customer poisoning, not operator edits, and nothing specifies where audit samples come from.
- Fix: Source outcome labels first from independent feeds (outbox post-install customer survey, AG/CFPB/BBB complaint feeds, lender disputes and delinquency, cancellation API logs, utility PTO records), which override CRM fields. Make CRM outcome fields append-only with signed, attributed edits and two-person approval for reclassifications or backdates. Sample suitability audits from the e-sign webhook list of all signed contracts. Raise a Tier A alarm on divergence between CRM and independent complaint rates per installer, and decouple ops and installer bonuses from training labels.
