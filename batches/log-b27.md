
### Round 364. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Speculation shaves the wrong latency: the endpointing silence timeout, not Needle or TTS, sets the reply gap, and Tier A states lengthen it
- Attack: Caller: "How long is the warranty?" The endpointer must see roughly 500-800 ms of silence before it declares end of turn, then the final ASR pass, GLOBAL and LOCAL passes and the cache-equality check run. The speculative audio is ready but sits unused until all of that finishes. In Tier A states (read-back, commit) the endpointer waits longer still. Every answer arrives one to two seconds after the caller stops. That is the "is anyone there?" range, so callers re-speak into the agent's reply. Across 10 languages, human turn gaps cluster at about 0-200 ms (Stivers et al. 2009, PNAS). Production endpointers default to hundreds of ms of trailing silence (Deepgram/Telnyx endpointing docs). Speculation removes compute time but cannot remove the silence wait that comes before the equality check. "Feeling human" can't happen by design, and the slowest gaps fall on the most important turns.
- Defense: The attack assumes a generic vendor endpointer at a fixed 500-800 ms. Ours is not that. R25 is "semantic endpointing": the incomplete() tool runs in every state, plus a dangling-word rule, with the "threshold short when complete". A turn that Needle scores complete on the prefix ("How long is the warranty?") ends on the short threshold, not the vendor default. R65 says timer values are "fit from ASR word timestamps in real calls and A/B tested", and R100 measures "per-state hit rate and p95 reply latency". The longer Tier A wait is deliberate: the design never promised 200 ms gaps on commit turns. It chose to be slower and safer there. Where the gap hurts, the measured p95 shows it per state.
- Verdict: The attack assumes a fixed vendor endpointer at 500-800 ms, but the design already has semantic endpointing (R25: incomplete() in every state plus a dangling-word rule, "threshold short when complete"), and timers fit from real calls and A/B tested (R65). After the endpoint, the remaining work is two passes of a ~26M model and a C transition. The longer Tier A wait is a deliberate safety trade, and R100 already measures per-state p95 reply latency.

### Round 365. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Cache misses land on objections and money questions, so the agent's delays fall exactly where human listeners read delay as evasion or bad news
- Attack: Routine turns ("yes, I own it") hit the cache, and the agent answers almost at once. "Wait, is there a lien on my house?" or "What happens if I sell?" are rare, so they miss the cache and are computed fresh with a visibly longer gap. Speculation also switches off under "rising confusion." So the agent hesitates only on the lien, cancellation and price questions. The caller hears a rep who stalls on the hard questions and decides the agent is hiding something, or that the answer is bad. Gap length carries meaning. Dispreferred responses come later, and listeners revise their expectations toward "no" or "trouble" as the silence grows (Kendrick & Torreira 2015; Bögels et al.). Hit-or-miss latency is a deterministic, involuntary prosodic signal that lines up with the compliance-critical topics. It was never reviewed as part of the approved message.
- Defense: A cache miss costs far less than the attack thinks. Lien, cancellation and price answers are fixed policy-table fragments: "every fact token is a table key", and R105 has us "pre-render every fact-bearing clip at build time with an audio hash". So a miss needs no TTS. It needs only the two passes of a ~26M encoder-decoder and a C transition. The fresh path is tens of milliseconds, not a hesitation a listener notices. Speculation is also off "in states where measured hit rate/p95 latency don't pay" (R100), so in those states every turn takes the same fresh path. Turning speculation off under rising confusion (R15) slows answers to every confused turn, not only to the money questions.
- Verdict: The existing design already removes the timing signal the attack relies on: R301 quantizes reply onset to a fixed post-endpoint delay bucket per state "whether speculation hit, missed or clarified", with a timing-indistinguishability CI test. A lien or cancellation question therefore can't be audibly slower than a routine turn in the same state. Fact answers are also pre-rendered at build time (R105), so a miss adds no TTS time.

### Round 366. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Safety exits are never pre-rendered: stop, cancel and request_human are rare, so they are never in top-k and always take the slowest path
- Attack: Speculation ranks the state's likely outcomes on the partial transcript and pre-renders the top-k, which are always LOCAL sales transitions. Caller: "Look, just take me off your list." The cache misses, the turn is computed fresh, and the opt-out confirmation plays after the longest gap in the call. Meanwhile the caller has said "Hello? Did you hear me?" or hung up. A hang-up means the confirmation and the outbox copy go out to someone who has left, and the next turn's state is undefined. Hit rate is optimized per state, so the cache structurally favors sales transitions and penalizes every GLOBAL safety event. The case that most needs a prompt acknowledgment (a revocation that must be honored, a person in distress) is the one guaranteed the worst latency. It also produces the "Hello?" overlap turns that the commit and stop logic handle worst.
- Defense: Safety exits don't go through the speculation cache. The stop path is "union of deterministic lexicon (incl. dialect patterns) and Needle GLOBAL". Under R24, Tier 1 phrases such as "take me off" mean "end immediately". The lexicon pre-empts sensitive events too (R19). Exit audio is a fixed fragment, certified and pre-rendered (R105), and is not rendered on demand. GLOBAL runs on every turn whatever the speculation state, and R192 proves that the root handler is the effective one for every state and GLOBAL event. A hang-up is "out-of-band telephony events handled at the StateSmith root", so the next state is defined, and R173's POST_CALL handler takes any late ASR. The DNC write and the outbox copy are meant to happen even if the caller has gone: a written confirmation isn't wasted on someone who hung up.
- Verdict: Under R301 the onset of a stop or cancel reply is the same fixed bucket as a cache hit, so safety exits are not "guaranteed the worst latency". Tier 1 stop phrases are a deterministic lexicon that ends the call immediately (R24), GLOBAL runs every turn, and hang-ups are root telephony events with a POST_CALL handler (R173), so the next state is defined. The DNC write and the written confirmation are meant to happen even after a hang-up.

### Round 367. Winner: Designer
- Topic: The longer Tier A endpoint wait after the closing question copies the "silent close" pressure tactic
- Attack: The agent asks the read-back confirmation question and then waits longer than anywhere else in the call. A hesitant owner pauses to think. The silence stretches, the social pressure to fill it builds, and they fill it with "...uh, yeah, okay I guess." That trailing "okay" is short, direct and lexical, so it passes COMMIT. The extra wait meant for safety has given the agent, at the decisive moment, the oldest pressure move in sales training: "after you ask for the order, shut up." A deliberate long silence at the close is a known high-pressure technique. People rush to fill gaps that pass about a second because silence signals trouble (Roberts et al. 2006; Kendrick & Torreira 2015). Fixed by graph, it is applied to every hesitant caller, and the agent can't say "take your time" because no event signals hesitation.
- Defense: The longer Tier A wait is the end-of-turn threshold, which applies after the caller has started speaking (R10, R25). It isn't a programmed silence after the question. The pre-answer pause is governed separately by R65's "PAUSE substate after the ask" and silence(duration_bucket) events. And "uh, yeah, okay I guess" can't close. R144 makes the commit "an action-production prompt ('please say send the contract'), not a yes/no". R77 requires "a narrow unambiguous set", and anything else gets a closed re-ask. That re-ask "ends on the safe option ('…, or not?')" (R239-1). And "any re-ask at COMMIT means no same-call close and a written proposal with cooling-off" (R239-3). Filling the silence buys a cooling-off period, not a sale.
- Verdict: The pressured "uh, yeah, okay I guess" cannot close the sale in the existing design: R144 requires the action phrase "send the contract", not a yes, R77 limits commits to a narrow affirm set, and any re-ask at COMMIT means no same-call close and a written proposal with cooling-off (R239-3). The silence may make the caller uncomfortable, but it cannot turn that discomfort into a contract.

### Round 368. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: The agent never backchannels, so on a phone line its silence during long caller turns sounds like a dropped call
- Attack: A caller explains their roof history for 25 seconds. On the phone there are no visual cues, and the agent has no "mm-hm" fragment: it only labels finished turns. After about eight seconds of nothing the caller stops: "Hello? You still there?" That splits their account into fragments. The endpointer closes a turn mid-story, speculation resets, and "you still there?" becomes a LOCAL turn (small_talk or unclear). The remainder of the roof story arrives as a new turn the agent answers out of context. Telephone talk depends on regular verbal backchannels, because listeners use them to show the channel is alive. Speakers who get none falter, repeat themselves and check the line. The design's own vocabulary treats backchannels only as a caller-side risk. It has no way to give a continuer without "talking over the caller", so long, important turns (the ones that matter for suitability) get broken up.
- Defense: The scenario assumes the story gets chopped. It doesn't. R25 holds the turn open for "~2-3s when incomplete", so mid-story pauses don't close it. R44's long-turn pipeline ("deterministic segmenter -> per-segment Needle -> merger with self-repair rules") is built for exactly this 25-second history, and it ends with a "summary read-back after multi-fact turns" that rejoins anything the caller split. Long turns are "a separate Tier A stratum" with speculation skipped, so no reset races happen. Even so, there is a real gap: the agent has no continuer of its own.
- Verdict: The defense agrees that the agent has no continuer, and its fix (continuers, a channel_check event) is entirely new. The existing pieces don't close the gap. The R44 merger joins segments inside one turn, but a caller's "you still there?" ends that turn, so the rest of the story arrives as a new turn. Worse, R107 treats repeated "hello?" as a media fault and hangs up. On a phone line, 20+ seconds of total silence from the listener reliably makes callers check the line, which breaks up the suitability answers that matter most.
- Fix: Add a certified continuer set ("mm-hm", "I see", "go on", no assent words) that plays only in intra-turn pauses scored non-final by incomplete(), after about 6 s of caller speech, never over voiced frames and never in AWAIT_CONFIRM. Add a channel_check event that answers "Yes, I'm here, go ahead" and keeps the long turn open so the R44 merger joins both sides. Exclude channel_check from the R107 "hello?" counter, and add a fragmentation-rate bound on a long-narrative stratum.

### Round 369. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Barge-in and backchannel are not told apart during agent playback, so habitual "mm-hm"s chop every fragment
- Attack: A caller who backchannels heavily says "mm-hm", "right", "yeah" every few seconds, as many speakers do (Japanese aizuchi, call-and-response styles, older speakers). Each one is voiced energy while the agent is playing. If that counts as barge-in, playback stops, the speculation cache is thrown away, and mandatory segments, which count only on uninterrupted playback, restart. The preamble and READ_BACK never finish. If it doesn't count, a real "wait, stop" said with the same short shape is ignored until the fragment ends. The design says "Barge-in discards" but defines no rule for what counts as barge-in over agent speech. Either threshold fails a group systematically. Japanese conversation has backchannels roughly three times as often as English (Maynard). The failure falls by culture and dialect, the very strata the per-group release blockers exist to protect.
- Defense: The design does define barge-in, contrary to the attack. R108: "any near-end energy burst during agent playback … stops playout and enters PAUSE". R14 then classifies the residue: "Short affirmations overlapping agent speech = backchannel, no event". So "wait, stop" can never be ignored: every burst yields, and stop-adjacent residue "triggers the R24 check question at once". The safe horn is the one we chose. The cost lands on heavy backchannelers, whose mandatory segments replay (R39). That costs time but never harms them, and the per-group strata exist to measure exactly that cost.
- Verdict: The design does define barge-in, but that rule produces the attack's first horn: R108 stops playout on any near-end burst and says "Sorry, go ahead", and R39 replays an interrupted mandatory segment from the start. A caller who says "mm-hm" every few seconds can therefore stop the preamble or READ_BACK from ever completing, and the agent keeps apologising. That is a systematic failure for heavy-backchannel dialects and age groups, the very groups the per-group bounds protect. The defense's fix (duck-then-decide) is new.
- Fix: Duck-then-decide: a burst during playback ducks audio and holds it for up to about 400 ms while the lexicon classifies the residue. A short backchannel with no stop-adjacent token resumes from the current clause and keeps the delivered flag. Anything else stops playout and goes to PAUSE as now. After 3 backchannel overlaps, switch to short chunks with pauses (the R238 profile). Backchannel overlaps don't count toward the R152 barge-in signal. Add a high-backchannel stratum with per-group completion and replay bounds as release blockers.

### Round 370. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Simultaneous-start collisions: after a gap, the caller and the agent start together, both stop, and the agent restarts its fragment from the top
- Attack: After a cache miss the agent begins its reply 1.2 s late, just as the caller starts "So what about—". Barge-in stops the agent. The caller, being polite, also stops at the overlap. Now there is mutual silence. The endpointer sees a three-word fragment, Needle labels it unclear, and the agent replays its earlier reply from the start, again overlapping the caller's restart. Three rounds of "sorry, go ahead" follow, each a turn that counts against the clarify-once budget, and the call falls back to a callback nobody asked for. Overlap resolution is a two-party protocol. Humans settle it with fine-grained timing and by one party "dropping out" mid-word, then letting the other go first. The design has only one rule (the agent yields and discards) and no "you first" fragment. Its restart-from-start playback makes the collision more likely to repeat, and each collision costs budget and pushes the call toward fallback.
- Defense: The "you first" fragment exists. R108's yield enters PAUSE with "Sorry, go ahead", and PAUSE is listed among the "internal transitions" (small_talk, acknowledge, side_talk, HOLD, PAUSE). So a collision doesn't spend the clarify budget. Restart-from-top applies only to mandatory segments (R39). An ordinary reply resumes "at the current question (re-read it)" (R24 resume window). Budget counters (R69) end a pathological loop in WRAP_UP, not in an endless round of apologies. Admittedly, the third-round collision and the "unclear" label on a truncated three-word start are real.
- Verdict: The "you first" move the attack says is missing already exists: R108 yields into PAUSE with "Sorry, go ahead", and PAUSE is an internal transition that doesn't spend the clarify budget. A truncated "So what about—" ends on a dangling word, so R25's incomplete() holds the turn open for 2-3 s and the caller's restart joins the same turn instead of being labeled unclear. Ordinary replies resume at the current question (R24), and R69 budgets bound any loop.

### Round 371. Winner: Designer
- Topic: A barge-in on a half-played question binds the caller's answer to a question they never fully heard, especially "A or B" questions
- Attack: QUALIFY: "Do you own the home, or are you currently renting with—" The caller cuts in at "home": "Yeah." Playback stops, but the turn is still answered against the state's question id, so ownership = yes. The same happens with "Would you like the 8 kilowatt system, or the smaller 6 with—", where "yeah" binds to the first option. The commit-time ban ("no commit tools while agent audio plays") doesn't apply because playback stopped before the caller answered. The slot was grounded on the word "yeah". Callers answer early at projected completion points. An alternative question projects completion after the first alternative, so a "yes" there answers a yes/no question that was never asked. The design records which fragment was asked, not how much of it played. Provenance says "spoken", but the caller never heard the alternative they rejected.
- Defense: The binding the attack describes can't happen. R14: "AWAIT_* entered only when the question finishes playing", with "Separate *_SPEAKING states while agent audio plays". A "Yeah" at "home" overlaps agent speech, so it is "backchannel, no event", and R25 says "Overlapped text within agent playback window excluded". R106 extends that window by the path delay, and answers in the guard band "count as overlap and get a closed re-ask". If the caller keeps talking, R108 yields to PAUSE and the question is re-read whole (R24 resume). Qualification-critical slots are Tier A, and R287 requires "an explicit lexical affirm at the critical-slot read-back". System size comes from the calculator, and every commit passes READ_BACK.
- Verdict: R14 says AWAIT_* states are entered only when the question finishes playing, and an affirmation overlapping agent speech is a backchannel with no event. R25 excludes overlapped text and R106 extends that window by path delay, with a closed re-ask in the guard band. A "Yeah" at "home" therefore can't write the ownership slot, and system size comes from the calculator and goes through READ_BACK anyway.

### Round 372. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Trail-off turns ("but...", "so...", "I mean...") are finished turns, but dangling-word endpointing treats them as incomplete(), so caller and agent both wait
- Attack: Read-back. Caller: "Well, it sounds good, but..." and stops: a polite refusal that leaves the floor to the agent. The turn ends on a dangling conjunction, so incomplete() fires and the endpointer waits longer, as Tier A states do. The caller is waiting too. After several seconds of silence the caller fills the gap with "...yeah," which joins the turn or starts a new one. "It sounds good ... yeah" now reaches a commit-shaped decision instead of the implied no. Turn-final "but" and "so" are recognized floor-yielding devices in conversation (studies of trail-off conjunctions; Walker 2012). They do the most work in indirect refusals. Treating them as syntactically unfinished guarantees dead air at exactly the moment the caller is softly declining, and it invites the filler that the lexical COMMIT gate accepts.
- Defense: "It sounds good, but..." doesn't end in a commit. R144 requires the caller to produce "send the contract", and a late "yeah" isn't that. A turn with "but" is neither "short, direct" nor free of conditions, so it gets a closed re-ask. That re-ask ends on "…, or not?" (R239-1), and any re-ask at COMMIT means "no same-call close" (R239-3). Under R289, a hedge carrying a condition becomes condition_stated, which also blocks the close. After the incomplete() wait ends, Needle labels the trailing turn, and R145's indirect_decline class ("high-context indirect refusals") is trained on exactly this kind of refusal. So the soft no is honoured, just two seconds late.
- Verdict: The harm the attack describes, a filler "yeah" being accepted as a commit, is blocked: R144 needs the caller to say "send the contract", R77 rejects conditional and hedged turns, and any re-ask at COMMIT means no same-call close (R239-3). R145 indirect_decline and R289 condition_stated also catch "sounds good, but...". What's left is a couple of seconds of dead air before a soft no is honoured, which is a cost to the call's feel, not a broken safeguard.

### Round 373. Winner: Designer
- Topic: The "speculation on/off gives bit-identical committed traces" CI check is open-loop, so it cannot see how reply timing changes what the caller says next
- Attack: CI replays recorded caller audio twice, with speculation on and off, and gets identical traces. In live calls, speculation changes when the agent starts speaking, and that changes the caller. With speculation off the reply comes 900 ms later, so the caller adds "...and is that with the battery?" or says "Hello?" The audio, the endpoint, the event set and the path are all different. The check passes because recorded audio can't react to the agent's timing. Turn-taking is closed-loop: response latency causally shapes the next turn (Stivers 2009; Kendrick & Torreira 2015). Bit-identical replay over fixed audio proves only that the computation is the same, never that the behavior is safe. The certification claim that speculation is a "cache only" with no behavioral effect is untestable by the stated CI. It needs live, timing-stratified audited calls, which the harm gate doesn't break out by speculation mode.
- Defense: R64's CI check claims isolation, not behavioural equivalence. It proves that "live slots and backtrack history" are "written only on commit", so a guess can't leak into state. The closed-loop safety claim never rested on it. It rests on the release gate: a "per-call harm bound measured only on real calls labeled by humans listening to audio", with "continuous audited sampling with per-state drift alarms". Real calls already include whatever timing speculation produces, closed loop included. R100 measures p95 latency per state and turns speculation off where it doesn't pay. R65's timers are "A/B tested" on live calls. The attack asks for live timing evidence, and the design already gathers it.
- Verdict: The bit-identical CI test (R64) claims only that speculation can't leak into state. The closed-loop safety claim rests on the audited real-call harm gate, which already includes whatever timing speculation produces. The attack's scenario (speculation off makes the reply 900 ms later) is also largely closed by R301, which releases reply onset at the same fixed per-state bucket whether speculation hit, missed or was off.
