
### Round 374. Winner: Designer
- Topic: The symmetric near-tie rule treats a stop-vs-continue tie like any other tie, so a probable opt-out gets "could you say that again?" instead of a stop
- Attack: Over a noisy line the caller says "yeah no, I'm done, just quit calling here." Lexicon misses it ("quit calling" isn't listed, and ASR writes "quick calling"). GLOBAL scores stop 0.46, acknowledge 0.41: a near-tie, so clarify. Agent: "Sorry, could you say that again?" The caller, annoyed, says "I said I'm good." That now reads as acknowledge or polite decline, and the pitch continues. Stop is caught later in the post-call sweep, if at all. Clarify-on-margin is cost-blind: a missed stop and a wrong roof size are not equal errors. Under 47 CFR 64.1200(a)(10) (2024 order), revocation by "any reasonable means" must be honored, and "quit" is a listed per-se revocation word. Asking the caller to repeat a likely revocation means continuing a sales call after revocation. A Bayes decision rule would choose stop on ties.
- Defense: The scenario uses the wrong rule. Top-1/top-2 clarify applies to LOCAL. GLOBAL is multi-label (R189): "each safety event gets its own calibrated threshold and every event above it fires." There's no argmax tie, so stop at 0.46 fires whenever it clears its own threshold. R133 sets that threshold to hit a target false-continue rate, which makes the rule cost-weighted, not symmetric. Even below threshold, the follow-up "I said I'm good" gets R145 indirect_decline: "Would you prefer we don't call again?" Anything except an explicit time request suppresses the lead. R108 sends stop-adjacent tokens ("calling") straight to the R24 check question. R169 and the post-call sweep hold the number until a human reviews it. The attack is right on one point: a generic "say that again?" should never be the reply to stop mass.
- Verdict: The scenario assumes GLOBAL is an argmax with a stop-vs-acknowledge tie, but R189 (in design.md) made GLOBAL multi-label with a per-event calibrated threshold, and acknowledge is not a GLOBAL event, so no stop tie exists there. R133 sets those thresholds against a target false-continue rate, which is already the cost-weighted rule the attack asks for, and R145 indirect_decline, R190's safety-mass veto, R66 sweep and R169 lock back it up.

### Round 375. Winner: Designer
- Topic: The clarify re-ask produces hyperarticulated repetition that the pinned ASR recognizes worse, so "clarify once" makes the second try less likely to succeed than the first
- Attack: The ASR confidence pre-gate rejects "fifteen years." The agent re-asks. Like most people correcting a machine, the caller slows down, separates the syllables, and stresses the vowels: "FIF... TEEN... YEARS." The telephony ASR, trained on conversational speech, misreads the stretched words or scores them lower. The second try fails too, so the call goes to the callback or human fallback. Accented and older speakers hyperarticulate the most. Oviatt et al. (JASA 1998, CHAM model) show users hyperarticulate after recognition errors: longer duration, more pauses, more careful articulation. This shifts the input away from the recognizer's training distribution and raises error rates. The design's one retry is drawn from a worse distribution than the original turn, and nothing in training covers it, since synthetic "ASR noise" doesn't model error-recovery speech. https://faculty.washington.edu/levow/papers/JASA98.pdf
- Defense: The design never assumes the retry comes from the same distribution. R20 certifies on the agent's own on-policy calls, so every real post-clarify turn, hyperarticulated ones included, is in the human-audited gold and counts toward the bound. R277 fits the confusion model on real audited calls rather than LLM-made noise. Numbers already have a non-acoustic route: R26 confirms Tier A numbers digit-wise, and R111/R121 offer DTMF and SMS/web paths. When the second try fails, the failure is bounded: R111 counts it as an access failure, R121 sends it to a human, and per-group fallback and false-unintelligible rates block release. The attack does identify an unmeasured stratum: the second try is its own distribution and should be measured as one.
- Verdict: R20 certifies on the agent's own on-policy calls and R277 fits its confusion model on real audited calls, so post-clarify speech is in the measured data, not assumed away. A failed second try lands in R111/R121 access routing (DTMF, text/web, human) with per-group fallback rates as release blockers, which is a safe, bounded outcome rather than a harm.

### Round 376. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Model uncertainty is billed to the caller: Needle's own near-ties and clarifies are indistinguishable from caller confusion, so they drive "rising confusion" and the vulnerability counter
- Attack: A lucid owner speaks African American English. Needle, under-trained on that dialect, near-ties three times in QUALIFY. Each clarify is logged as a "confusion" signal. Speculation turns off ("rising confusion"), replies get slower, the vulnerability counter crosses its threshold, and the call goes to NO_SALE_SAFE_EXIT. The CRM now flags this caller as vulnerable or confused. Another caller, who really is confused but whose speech is fluent and in-distribution, never triggers a clarify and gets the close. The gates see one signal, a low margin, that mixes up epistemic uncertainty (the model's) with caller state (aleatoric). The deterministic counters count the model's ignorance as evidence about the person. This produces both errors at once: lucid out-of-distribution speakers are labeled vulnerable and turned away, and truly confused in-distribution speakers sail through.
- Defense: R111 already removes most of this: "Exclude unintelligible, low-ASR-confidence and pure-ASR-quality unclear events from the vulnerability counter." It also makes per-group false-vulnerability and fallback rates release blockers. A model that near-ties three times on AAE gives that stratum a high false-vulnerability rate, and the release fails. R275 runs matched-pair SAE/AAE tests and bars teacher labels for any stratum with disparity. The CRM never sees the flag: R203's handoff schema "never carries the vulnerability counter," and the flag only routes. The truly confused fluent caller still hits R15 teach-back, the R144 action-production commit, R152 overcommitment signals and R239's echo filter, none of which depend on margin. The remaining gap is near-tie clarifies, which R111 doesn't name.
- Verdict: R15's vulnerability counter counts unclear()/clarify events, and R111 only excludes unintelligible, low-ASR-confidence and pure-ASR-quality unclear events; a Needle margin near-tie on clean AAE audio is none of those, so model ignorance still drives a lucid caller to NO_SALE_SAFE_EXIT with redial suppressed. Per-group false-vulnerability blockers (R111) and R275 matched pairs catch this only in aggregate at release, and the defense itself names the near-tie gap; the CRM-flag part of the attack is wrong (R203), but the per-call conflation stands.
- Fix: The vulnerability counter takes only caller-content signals (confused(), contradictions between committed slots, wrong_party_reference, R152 overcommitment). Margin near-ties, OOD-floor unclear and grounding misses go to a separate model_uncertainty counter that routes to a human or ACCESS_PATH as an access failure, never to NO_SALE_SAFE_EXIT, never suppresses redial and never disables speculation as a "confusion" signal.

### Round 377. Winner: Designer
- Topic: The "live error bound" can only run on real-time confidence proxies (clarify and near-tie rates), and those proxies reward confidently wrong states and punish careful ones
- Attack: Human audit labels arrive days later, so the live per-state error bound has to use what is available in real time: clarify rate, near-tie rate, fallback rate. After a release, state QUOTE becomes overconfident on "fifty vs fifteen." It almost never clarifies and looks healthy. State IDENTITY becomes more cautious, clarifies more, trips its bound, and goes to fallback. Ops reads this as "QUOTE is our best state." Its errors only show up weeks later in audits. This is Goodhart's law in the safety loop. Confident errors are by definition the ones that produce no uncertainty signal. A bound computed from self-reported uncertainty systematically clears the dangerous failure mode and penalizes the safe one. "Live" bounds built on the model's own confidence cannot detect a calibration collapse, the very failure they exist to catch.
- Defense: The live monitors are not just self-reported confidence. R132 also monitors per-state event-mix shift, lexicon-vs-Needle disagreement and hang-up-after-turn rate, all with CUSUM. CUSUM is two-sided, so a release that suddenly cuts QUOTE's clarify rate is itself a shift alarm and triggers a 100% audit surge. Several gates don't depend on Needle's confidence: the grounding gate, R186's role-binding and negation cross-check, R211's permit and aerial cross-checks, and R72/R156, under which QUOTE figures come only from verified_usage. A calibration collapse must also get through pre-release gates first: R3 gold replay, sealed holdouts and the R133 calibration split. R324 makes any rise in fallback rate a blocker, so ops can't read "cautious" as "bad" without that being audited. Overconfidence does need its own named signal.
- Verdict: The live monitors are not only self-reported confidence: R132 runs CUSUM on event-mix shift, lexicon-vs-Needle disagreement and hang-up-after-turn rate with audit surges, and a release-induced overconfidence on fifty/fifteen must first pass R3 human-audited gold replay, sealed holdouts and the R133 calibration split, plus R26 digit-wise confirmation of Tier A numbers. Confident errors are caught by label-based pre-release gates and continuous audited sampling, not by clarify rates.

### Round 378. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: The gate thresholds themselves (margin, softmax floor, ASR-confidence cutoff, vulnerability count, call budgets) aren't listed in the content-hashed certified bundle, so they can be changed without recertifying
- Attack: The bundle hashes graph, weights, fragments, tool lists, ASR and policy table. Conversion drops 8% after a release because clarifies are up. A growth engineer lowers the near-tie margin from 0.15 to 0.08 and the ASR-confidence floor from 0.6 to 0.45 in runtime config. The hash, certificate and replay are all unchanged. Clarifies fall, commits rise, and confidently wrong stops and commits pass through under a certificate measured at the old operating point. Every error rate in the certificate is conditional on a threshold, because thresholds set the point on the risk-coverage curve. If the thresholds aren't part of the certified artifact, the certificate certifies a model, not a decision rule. Bit-identical replay with the new config reproduces the new behavior faithfully, so replay can't flag the change either.
- Defense: The design treats thresholds as certified outputs. R133 re-sets "margin, OOD and clarify thresholds" on a held-out calibration split each release to hit target false-stop and false-commit rates, so a threshold is a release artifact. R273 logs "mask/threshold version" on every turn's decision record, so a change shows up in every audited trace. R328 already puts alarm thresholds in "the content-hashed certified bundle," and changing one is a release with two-person sign-off. R324 names this exact move: "lowering the clarify threshold to shed hard calls fails release." R184 replays margin decisions bitwise. The final-plan bundle list doesn't name the gate thresholds, though, so that should be stated explicitly.
- Verdict: R133 re-sets thresholds per release and R273 logs the threshold version, but nothing in design.md puts the gate thresholds into the content-hashed bundle or certificate key: R53/R3 key on weights, runtime, hardware and facts table, and R328 covers only alarm thresholds. A runtime config change therefore leaves the certificate valid while moving the operating point it was measured at; logging detects it later, it does not prevent it.
- Fix: Make every gate parameter (margin, softmax/OOD floor, ASR and word-confidence floors, veto threshold, vulnerability counts, call budgets, endpointing timers) a compile-time constant in the generated C, included in the bundle hash and certificate key, with no runtime config path and a build check that fails if any guard reads a non-hashed value.

### Round 379. Winner: Designer
- Topic: The ASR-confidence pre-gate scores whole utterances, so a confident average hides the one low-confidence word that decides the turn
- Attack: "Yeah, the house is in my name and my wife's, and we can't do anything over a hundred a month." Ten clear words, one garbled: "can't" comes out as "can" with word-confidence 0.31. The utterance's mean confidence is 0.88 and passes the pre-gate. Needle sees "we can do anything over a hundred a month," grounds budget > 100, and is confident. No gate re-asks, because the gate measures how clear the turn was, not how clear its pivotal token was. Utterance-level confidence is an average. Negations, digits and modals are short, unstressed, and carry the most information. A gate keyed to mean confidence approves exactly the turns where one critical word is a guess among many clear ones. The grounding gate then confirms the misheard word, since it appears in the "final ASR text."
- Defense: Several existing gates work below utterance level. R186's extractor tags negation scope per token, and a disagreement with Needle's binding goes to clarify. R104 already checks commit tokens one by one against ASR word timestamps. R26 confirms Tier A numbers digit-wise. R273 keeps the ASR n-best per turn. The pivotal error here also does limited harm: R72 says "spoken amounts only fill coarse routing bands," and binding terms come only from the price table, verified_usage and the READ_BACK plus action-production commit (R144). A misheard budget can't create a contract. The attack is right that the pre-gate averages, so a single low-confidence pivotal token should trigger a re-ask on its own.
- Verdict: A garbled "can't" can mis-set a budget slot, but R72 keeps spoken amounts in coarse routing bands only, R2 reads back every qualification-critical slot before a transition depends on it, and binding terms come only from the price table, verified usage and READ_BACK plus the R144 action-production commit. So one misheard pivotal word becomes a caught correction or a band misroute, not a contract harm.

### Round 380. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Fallback to a booked callback sends the caller back into the same uncertain state with the same weights, which creates a clarify-fail-callback loop that burns frequency caps and consent
- Attack: QUALIFY can't parse a caller's answers about roof ownership (an accent plus a mixed-title situation), and after one clarify it falls back to "We'll call you back." The callback is placed by the same bundle, which runs the preamble and identity again and reaches the same QUALIFY turn. It hits the same near-tie, clarifies once, and books another callback. After three calls the caller is angry, hasn't refused anything, and hasn't been told that a machine can't understand them. "Deterministic fallback" is only a fallback if it changes something. A callback to the same deterministic agent is a retry with no new information, and determinism guarantees the same failure. Nothing records "this caller failed state X," so the frequency caps and repeat-call harassment rules (TSR 310.4(b)(1)(i)) are spent on a loop the design itself creates.
- Defense: R169 already rules out this loop: "Any call that ends on unintelligible fallback books nothing." R121 says that a call with repeated unintelligible turns but clear speech "is never given an AI callback, and its lead is flagged human-only." R111 routes repeated re-asks to the ACCESS_PATH (DTMF, SMS/web or a human with extra time). R293 sends budget exhaustion to request_human, never a generic follow-up. R284 forbids any callback after a hostile turn, and R145 suppresses on defers. So the second call in the scenario goes to a person, not to the same bundle. The gap is that a near-tie fallback (not unintelligible) isn't explicitly tied to a lead-level memory.
- Verdict: R169 and R121 stop the loop only for unintelligible fallback; a near-tie clarify-then-fallback in QUALIFY can still end in a booked callback by the same bundle, and nothing in design.md records per lead which state failed or bars the AI from re-entering it. Frequency caps (R41) and the R284 hostility exit only end the loop after the caller has been called repeatedly and angered, which is the harm.
- Fix: Write a lead-level failed_state record (state id plus bundle hash) on any clarify-then-fallback; the R138 dial permit refuses any AI redial that would re-enter that state under the same or an equivalent bundle, so follow-up is only a human, DTMF/SMS self-serve or a written proposal, and the fallback fragment says plainly that a person will follow up.

### Round 381. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: The slot store has no UNKNOWN value, so a caller's honest "I don't know" about roof age, shade or move horizon is forced into a typed value, a clarify loop, or a guard default
- Attack: QUALIFY: "How old is your roof?" The caller: "No idea, we bought the place six years ago, it was here then." Grounding finds "six years" in the text, so roof_age=6. Or nothing grounds, the agent clarifies once ("about how many years?"), and the caller guesses "maybe ten?", which the rising pitch excludes only at COMMIT, not here. Either way the suitability table receives a crisp number and returns a confident SUITABLE. Every uncertainty gate in the design is about whether the machine understood the caller, not whether the caller knows the answer. Two-valued slots plus pure-code guards can't represent a fact that is understood but unknown. So the suitability exit, the "honest" path, runs on fabricated precision, and the provenance tag "spoken" makes a guess look like a verified fact in the audit.
- Defense: The scenario's binding fails under existing gates. R186 tags each number with its nearest slot cue, and "six years" binds to "bought the place," not "roof," so the disagreement goes to clarify. R211 cross-checks roof age against permit records, R71 sends uncertain roofs to ROOF_FIRST ("inspection before any quote"), and R2 reads critical slots back before any transition depends on them. The design already uses unknown values elsewhere: R159 (unknown panel amperage means "price subject to survey" with ranges), R227/R319 (unknown means no figure) and R341 (unknown category means human review or NOT_SUITABLE, "never a default"). The attack is right that qualification slots need a caller-unknown value of their own.
- Verdict: R186 does stop the specific "six years" misbinding, but the existing slot model has no caller-unknown value: after "no idea" the design can only clarify for a number, so the caller's guess is stored as a crisp value with "spoken" provenance or the call falls to fallback. The UNKNOWN handling the defense cites (R159, R227, R319, R341) covers other slots and table enums, not QUALIFY answers, and R211 permit cross-checks only help where a record exists.
- Fix: Add a dont_know(slot) event and an UNKNOWN value for every QUALIFY slot, a caller_estimate provenance for hedged answers, and mandatory suitability-table rows for UNKNOWN and near-cutoff estimates that resolve via permit/aerial records to ROOF_FIRST or NOT_SUITABLE (build check enforces the rows); no clarify asks for a number after the caller says they don't know.

### Round 382. Winner: Designer
- Topic: One-hot training labels from the big model and from single annotators teach Needle that no utterance is ambiguous, so the near-tie rule rarely fires on genuinely ambiguous turns
- Attack: "I mean, I guess it makes sense" occurs thousands of times in synthetic data, each time with one hard label (acknowledge, or affirm, depending on the generator's prompt). Real annotators disagree 50/50 on it, but the training target is a one-hot vector, never 0.5/0.5. Needle learns to output 0.97 on whichever label dominates its sources. At COMMIT-adjacent states the margin gate sees 0.97 vs 0.02 and never clarifies on the turns humans find most ambiguous. Calibration has to be learned from label distributions, not imposed by thresholds. Peterson et al. (ICCV 2019, CIFAR-10H) show that training on human label distributions instead of hard labels improves calibration and robustness. The design trains on single labels per example and then trusts a softmax margin to show ambiguity it was trained to erase. https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07086
- Defense: The design doesn't rely on the margin alone to surface ambiguity. R77 gives ambiguity its own audio-informed ambiguous_polarity label class, which maps to clarify, so "I guess it makes sense" is trained toward a class that is explicitly ambiguous. R133 fits temperature scaling on a real audited split. R131 measures inter-rater agreement with two auditors, and R332 escalates codebook-ambiguous items. COMMIT can't fire on that utterance regardless: R144 requires an action-production phrase ("say 'send the contract'"), and R9 takes only a short lexical affirm. Hedges like this one are excluded. The point about hard labels is fair as a training improvement.
- Verdict: Ambiguity is not left to the margin alone: R77 trains an explicit audio-informed ambiguous_polarity class mapped to clarify, R133 calibrates on a real audited split, and an utterance like "I guess it makes sense" cannot commit anyway because R144 requires an action-production phrase and the COMMIT gate takes only a short lexical affirm. The overconfident hard-label harm the attack describes has no commit path.

### Round 383. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Explicit confirmation ("Did you say fifteen?") turns misrecognitions into "customer-confirmed" facts, because callers accept wrong echoes and the "yes" then becomes the provenance
- Attack: Grounding is uncertain on the monthly-bill amount, so the agent asks "Just to confirm, that's two hundred fifty a month?" The caller said "a hundred fifty." They half-hear the agent's TTS on a speakerphone and say "yeah." The closed binary re-ask accepts it. The slot now carries provenance "spoken" plus a confirmation record. Savings and system size are computed from $250, and in any later dispute the log shows the customer confirmed it. The uncertainty-resolution step has its own acceptance bias. People say "yes" to echoes they didn't fully process, especially for numbers, and the binary re-ask has no third option such as "no, it's...". Each confirmation creates audit evidence that blames the customer for the machine's misrecognition, which makes confidently wrong slots both more likely to survive and harder to challenge.
- Defense: The scenario's harm can't happen. R72 says "spoken amounts only fill coarse routing bands," and savings wording requires calculator_output. R156 says "the only writer for verified_usage is the utility data API or that extractor." Savings and size are never computed from $250 the caller said. R239's re-asks end on the safe option ("…, or not?"), and its echo filter discounts answers that match the prompt. R26/R22 confirm numbers digit by digit. R273 keeps the ASR n-best, so a dispute shows the original "one fifty" hypothesis, and the log doesn't blame the customer. Echo-confirmation as provenance is a real weakness for spoken bands and should change.
- Verdict: The scenario's harm cannot occur: R72 confines spoken amounts to coarse routing bands and R156 makes the utility API or bill extractor the only writer for verified_usage, so savings and size are never computed from a caller-confirmed $250. R239's safe-option re-asks and echo filter, R26/R22 digit-wise confirmation and R273's n-best decision record limit both the acceptance bias and the blame-the-customer audit trail.
