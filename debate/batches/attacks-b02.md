## A1
TOPIC: State do-not-call registries (Indiana, Texas, Colorado, Pennsylvania, Missouri) missing from the pre-dial gate
SCENARIO: 1) Indiana homeowner is on Indiana's state DNC list, not the national one. 2) A lead vendor form gave "prior express written consent" naming the seller and AI voice. 3) Pre-dial gate checks national + internal DNC, consent, RND, hours: all green. 4) Agent dials and closes a sale. 5) Homeowner complains to the Indiana Attorney General, who enforces its own list and its own, narrower exemptions. 6) Audit finds thousands of dials to state-list numbers, every one "certified compliant."
WHY IT BREAKS: The gate names only "national/internal DNC." Several states run separate registries with their own exemption wording that does not map onto federal PEWC (Indiana Code 24-4.7). "Most restrictive jurisdiction" is applied to hours and frequency, not to list scrubbing, so the model-checked invariant proves the wrong property. Source: in.gov/attorneygeneral do-not-call telephone-solicitors page.

## A2
TOPIC: Consent revoked through another channel (STOP reply to the e-sign SMS, email, installer, website) never reaching the dialer
SCENARIO: 1) Customer gets the e-sign envelope SMS, replies "STOP, no more calls." 2) The reply lands at the e-sign vendor's number, which only emits signature webhooks. 3) Customer also emails "remove me." 4) Neither is a voice transcript, so the post-call DNC sweep never sees them. 5) Eleven business days later the callback scheduler (WAITING_FOR_SIGNATURE follow-up) redials with the AI voice. 6) Customer sues for each call after revocation.
WHY IT BREAKS: Since April 11, 2025, 47 CFR 64.1200(a)(10) lets consumers revoke by any reasonable means, including replying to a text, and requires honoring it within 10 business days. The design's stop handling covers only voice: lexicon, Needle GLOBAL and the transcript sweep. Texts, emails and vendor channels it opens are not wired in. Source: FCC 24-24; Kelley Drye, Apr. 2025.

## A3
TOPIC: TSR 310.5 five-year recordkeeping vs "no-retain mode" and combinatorial fragment wordings
SCENARIO: 1) Privacy profile switches a California call to no-retain mode with PII surrogates. 2) Each utterance is built from fragments with "several wordings, chosen deterministically" plus slot fills. 3) FTC sends a civil investigative demand: produce the call detail record and the exact message played on call #8,413,227, plus the consent record. 4) Company has surrogate IDs, no audio, and must regenerate the message from a bundle hash that has since rolled back. 5) No authentic copy of what was actually played exists.
WHY IT BREAKS: The 2024 TSR amendment (effective Oct. 2024) requires sellers/telemarketers to keep for five years call detail records (number, time, duration, disposition, caller ID) and a copy of each unique prerecorded message, plus consent records. No-retain mode and per-call fragment variants break this. Source: 89 Fed. Reg. 26760 (Apr. 16, 2024); 16 CFR 310.5.

## A4
TOPIC: Litigation hold triggered mid-call ("my lawyer is recording this") while automated deletion keeps running
SCENARIO: 1) Callee says: "I've sued you people before, my attorney will hear this call." 2) No GLOBAL event for a legal threat, so Needle labels it out_of_scope or objection, and the call continues. 3) Standard retention jobs run: no-retain audio dropped, PII swapped for surrogates, the transcript scrubbed for training. 4) Three weeks later a TCPA class action is filed citing this call. 5) The company can't produce the original audio or unredacted transcript, only a "bit-identical replay" made from fragments.
WHY IT BREAKS: The duty to preserve starts when litigation is reasonably expected. Losing electronically stored information after that invites FRCP 37(e) sanctions, up to an adverse-inference instruction. A deterministic replay is a reconstruction, not the original record, so authentication (FRE 901) and the best-evidence rule (FRE 1002) can be attacked. The graph has no event or hold path for legal threats.

## A5
TOPIC: Illinois BIPA: speaker-change detection, self-echo filtering and prosody features as voiceprint collection without a written release
SCENARIO: 1) The agent calls an Illinois homeowner. 2) To run IDENTITY "speaker change" handling and the self-echo filter, the front end computes per-speaker voice embeddings and pitch features. 3) Its notices cover recording and AI, nothing more. 4) Embeddings are logged with the trace for audit and bit-identical replay. 5) A plaintiff firm subpoenas the pipeline spec and files a class action: voiceprints were collected from every Illinois callee without a signed written release or a published retention schedule.
WHY IT BREAKS: BIPA (740 ILCS 14/15(a)-(b)) requires a public retention policy and an informed written release before collecting a "voiceprint." Damages are $1,000 for negligent and $5,000 for reckless violations, with a private right of action. A spoken recording notice is not a written release. Texas CUBI imposes similar duties. The design never classifies its speaker-tracking features as biometric.

## A6
TOPIC: Washington My Health My Data Act: health inferences from sensitive_event and the vulnerability counter
SCENARIO: 1) A Washington callee says: "My wife's on home oxygen, we can't lose power." 2) The sensitive_event fires, the vulnerability counter goes up, and the trace is labeled "medical_dependency" for exit routing. 3) That labeled transcript goes into the audit sample and the per-release Needle finetune union. 4) No separate health-data consent was collected. 5) A consumer sues under the Washington Consumer Protection Act over the collection, the inference and the use in model training.
WHY IT BREAKS: Washington's MHMDA (RCW 19.373, in force since 2024) covers "consumer health data," including inferences from non-health data. It requires separate opt-in consent to collect, separate consent to share, and a health-data privacy policy, with a private right of action. The design labels, keeps and trains on exactly these inferences. Its PII surrogates hide names, not the health label.

## A7
TOPIC: ESIGN consumer-consent disclosure and inaccessible e-sign for a blind customer
SCENARIO: 1) A blind homeowner agrees by voice and gets the SMS envelope and OTP. 2) The vendor's consent-to-electronic-records screen and the contract PDF don't work with the phone's screen reader. 3) A sighted relative taps through "I agree to use electronic records" and signs. 4) The agent never confirmed the customer could open electronic records. 5) Customer later disputes the contract. The seller can't show valid electronic delivery of the required California solar disclosures or the senior cancellation notice.
WHY IT BREAKS: To satisfy a law requiring written disclosures, 15 USC 7001(c) needs the consumer's affirmative consent after hardware and software disclosures, given in a way that "reasonably demonstrates" they can access the records. If that fails, the disclosures were never legally given and cancellation windows may never start. Inaccessible signing also invites Unruh and ADA claims.

## A8
TOPIC: Disability discrimination against direct callers with speech disabilities (dysarthria, stuttering, laryngectomy)
SCENARIO: 1) A homeowner with Parkinson's dysarthria answers without a relay service. 2) The ASR-confidence pre-gate scores turns low and sends them to unintelligible re-ask. 3) The design is "clarify once, then fallback": after two turns the call ends with a callback that gets the same result. 4) Across the campaign, people with speech disabilities never reach QUOTE, while everyone else does. 5) A California consumer sues under the Unruh Act for being denied equal access to the offer.
WHY IT BREAKS: Unruh (Civ. Code 51, 52) covers business establishments, includes online and remote ones, and sets $4,000 minimum statutory damages per violation. The audit is split by dialect and accent, not disability. Relay handling covers TRS users only. The fixed clarify budget systematically excludes a protected class, with no accommodation path such as slower pacing, DTMF or text.

## A9
TOPIC: State telemarketer registration and bonding (Texas ch. 302 as amended by SB 140) as an unmodeled pre-dial requirement
SCENARIO: 1) A Texas campaign launches after September 1, 2025. 2) The seller holds no Texas Secretary of State telephone-solicitation registration or security deposit and relies on federal TCPA consent. 3) The SMS e-sign envelope and appointment texts now count as telephone solicitation under Texas law. 4) A recipient sues under the DTPA for each unregistered call and text. 5) The regulation profile has hours, DNC, consent and HIS rows, but no row requiring "registered and bonded in the callee's state."
WHY IT BREAKS: Texas SB 140 (effective September 1, 2025) extends ch. 302 to texts and makes violations actionable under the DTPA, adding private suits. Ch. 302 already required registration ($200 fee, $10,000 security). Other states, such as Utah and Washington, also require telemarketer registration. The gate never checks seller licensure status per state. Sources: Morgan Lewis (Sept. 2025); Kelley Drye (Sept. 2025).

## A10
TOPIC: E-signature attribution: OTP proves control of the phone, not identity, so one spouse signs for all record owners
SCENARIO: 1) Title lists two co-owners. 2) The agent captures the second owner's contact by asking the caller for it, and the caller gives their own second line. 3) Both OTP envelopes reach the same person, who signs as both. 4) Webhooks report "completed," the lien and UCC filing are recorded, and installation proceeds. 5) The non-signing spouse sues to void the contract and remove the lien, citing forgery. 6) The seller's only proof that the spouse signed is an OTP sent to a number the other spouse supplied.
WHY IT BREAKS: Under UETA §9, an e-signature binds a person only if it was their act, provable by security procedure. An OTP sent to a number given by an interested party proves nothing about who signed. The design checks how many record owners there are, not that each one independently authenticated. A forged co-owner signature voids the lien and exposes the seller to fraud claims.
