
### Round 304. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: E-sign OTP and "outbox copy" texts to a Canadian (+1) mobile fall under Canada's anti-spam law (CASL), which the compliance gates never model
- Attack: Ontario snowbird couple owns a Florida house. The husband passes IDENTITY on his US number. The wife, a record owner, has only her +1-905 Ontario mobile, so the envelope SMS goes there. The deterministic outbox also sends her the "copy" of contact info and DNC policy. It's NANP +1, so the dialer treats it as a domestic number. She gets SMS from a US solar seller with no CASL consent record, and no CASL identification or unsubscribe content in the fixed fragment. The design checks consent and SMS rules only under the TCPA and 10DLC. CASL covers commercial electronic messages sent to Canadian devices. It needs its own consent, sender identification and an unsubscribe mechanism, and penalties run up to C$10M. NANP +1 numbers make Canadian mobiles indistinguishable unless the NPA is checked, and the gate never checks it.
- Defense: The envelope cannot reach the wife's Ontario mobile by default. R149 extends "the consent gate to every outbound SMS and email" and says "never send a co-owner envelope to vendor-sourced contact data". She gets an envelope only if she "self-opts in via a link", and R123 requires each signer's "own consent or lead record". The "outbox copy" goes to the requesting verified caller, not to her. R122 counts "texts as solicitation", and SMS needs valid seller registration in every candidate jurisdiction. The real gap is that R196's "NANP mobile only" check treats +1 as domestic. CASL needs a country check that the NPA provides, plus identification and unsubscribe content.
- Verdict: R149, R123 and R122 are real and stop the default case, so an envelope never goes to vendor-sourced data. But once the wife opts in herself, the envelope SMS still goes to a +1-905 number. R196 explicitly treats all NANP mobiles as domestic, so no CASL profile is ever applied. CASL's s.6(6) transactional exemption removes only the consent requirement. The sender-identification and unsubscribe content are still required, and no fixed fragment carries them. The defense concedes the country check is missing and closes the gap only with NEW NPA resolution.
- Fix: Resolve every outbound number from NPA to country (with a carrier lookup) before any SMS or email effect. Treat Canadian and Caribbean NPAs as foreign jurisdictions. With no certified CASL profile, the outbox refuses and signing falls back to mail or consultant-assisted signing. Add a build check that every outbox fragment carries the profile's required identification and unsubscribe keys.

### Round 305. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Booked callbacks and warm-transfer follow-ups to Canadian numbers trigger the CRTC telemarketing regime (DNCL registration, Canadian calling hours, ADAD rules)
- Attack: A snowbird calls INBOUND from her Canadian cell. Mid-call she says "call me back Saturday evening, I'll be home in Ottawa." The callback is booked to a +1-613 number. The pre-dial gate runs the US national DNC, the RND and US calling hours for the "most restrictive candidate" state. At 7pm Saturday the agent dials an artificial-voice sales call into Canada. The CRTC Unsolicited Telecommunications Rules require registering with the National DNCL operator and paying fees before calling Canadians. Weekend calling must end at 6pm local time, and automatic dialing-announcing device (ADAD) solicitation needs express consent. None of this is in the regulation profile. Sources: crtc.gc.ca/eng/phone/telemarketing/tobligations/rules-regles.htm; Telecom Decision CRTC 2007-48.
- Defense: The callback never dials. "I'll be home in Ottawa" is a stated location change, so R70's jurisdiction_changed fires ("fired on any stated residence change, validated or not; unvalidated -> treat as added candidate"), and R183's current_location also "adds the stated place's time zone to the candidate set". Ontario becomes a candidate jurisdiction. Two invariants then block the dial: R114 ("no DIAL edge unless every candidate state's registry check passes", and "do not dial when ... its exemption can't be proven") and R122 ("no DIAL ... without valid registration in every candidate state"). With no DNCL row and no registration row for Canada, neither check can pass, so the result is fail-closed: no dial. The dialed +1-613 number makes the same point once NPA resolution exists.
- Verdict: R70 and R183 exist, but both are US-state-shaped. jurisdiction_changed takes a state, and current_location adds only a time zone, which for Ottawa is the same Eastern zone as Florida. The R114 and R122 "every candidate state" invariants are never shown to fail closed for a non-US candidate that has no rows at all. Nothing in the existing design models the CRTC weekend 18:00 cutoff, DNCL registration or ADAD consent. The +1-613 callback passes R196 as domestic. The fail-closed default for an unprofiled country is the defense's NEW addition.
- Fix: Any candidate jurisdiction without a certified regulation profile is a hard no-dial and no-SMS. This covers any non-US country, whether identified by NPA or by stated location. callback_request is refused at booking with a fixed fragment and a written follow-up instead. Add the invariant that no callback books to an unprofiled jurisdiction.

### Round 306. Winner: Designer
- Topic: US territories: Puerto Rico's net-metering law is under a federal oversight board's lawsuit, so a territory's "certified" policy-table row can be voided by PROMESA litigation, not by legislative change
- Attack: A 787 lead in Bayamón gets a QUOTE. The policy table cites Act 10-2024, which extends retail net metering in Puerto Rico, as the basis for savings. The fragment is certified, and the certificate expires only "when a cited policy row changes." Meanwhile the FOMB's suit to nullify Act 10 under PROMESA §204/§108 moves forward in federal court. A ruling or a regulator order lands, and no row edit ever happens. Certificate expiry keys on edits to table rows, not on legal events like an injunction or nullification. Territory law sits under a federal board that can void statutes, a layer no state profile has. Sources: FOMB Act 10 FAQ (oversightboard.pr.gov/act-10-faq); Canary Media on the FOMB lawsuit; KSAT (Jul 26 2024).
- Defense: The table isn't static. R157 adds a "tariff-filing monitor [that] expires rows". R140 makes the table forward-only, with "effective_from/until" windows that the runtime refuses to cross. R78 already has a "manual compliance override for lag" when legal events outrun feeds. Exposure is also small. R231 limits the call to "only year-one savings". R157 types future-rate rows as "assumption" provenance that must carry "estimate, assumes X, not guaranteed", and the default figure uses "today's rates only". The litigation risk falls on future NEM terms, which are never stated on the call as fact. The gap is that no monitor watches courts.
- Verdict: The attack assumes no row ever changes. But a ruling only alters what the customer gets once PREB or the utility changes the tariff, and the R157 tariff-filing monitor expires rows on exactly that. R78's manual compliance override covers lag. R231 and R157 limit the call to year-one savings at today's rates, and future NEM terms are only ever assumption-qualified. That residual risk is the same as any NEM change in any state.

### Round 307. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Day-of-week and legal-holiday telemarketing bans, including parish-only holidays and territory dates across the International Date Line, absent from a pre-dial gate built on hours and frequency
- Attack: The campaign runs on Mardi Gras Tuesday. In New Orleans that day is a legal holiday under Louisiana law, but only in certain parishes. Next weekend the dialer schedules Guam leads (UTC+10) for "Saturday 6pm Pacific," which is already Sunday in Guam. The gate checks the calling-hour window and frequency caps for the most restrictive candidate jurisdiction. It has no calendar-day rule and no parish-level holiday table. Louisiana's telephone-solicitation law (La. R.S. 45:844 series; LPSC DNC rules) bars calls on Sundays and state legal holidays, and Mardi Gras is a holiday only in listed parishes. Other states have similar Sunday or holiday bans. Rules keyed to the calendar date need the callee's local date and a sub-state holiday calendar. Sources: legis.la.gov RS 45:844.31; lpsc.louisiana.gov General Order R-35852.
- Defense: The gate already knows the callee's local date. R41 and R139 use "address tz" through "pinned tzdata" and property-test "every zone across DST boundaries", so Guam's Sunday is computed as Sunday, not as Pacific Saturday. R8 puts "state mini-TCPA rules as facts rows", and R70 applies "most-restrictive-wins" across candidates. A Louisiana Sunday ban is a mini-TCPA row. What's missing is a typed calendar rule and holiday scope below the state level, so Mardi Gras in only some parishes isn't expressible.
- Verdict: The local date is handled: R41 and R139 use address tz with pinned tzdata, so Guam's Sunday is computed correctly. R8 facts rows could hold a statewide Sunday ban. Louisiana's telephone-solicitation rules do bar Sunday and legal-holiday calls. But the existing gate has no typed calendar-day rule and no holiday scope below state level, so a parish-only Mardi Gras holiday cannot be expressed, which the defense concedes.
- Fix: Add calendar_rule rows (day-of-week bans and legal-holiday lists) to each profile. Give each holiday a geographic scope (state, parish or county, municipality) resolved from the parcel geocode, and treat unknown scope as statewide. Evaluate every candidate zone on its local date. Add a full-year property test and the invariant that no DIAL happens on a banned local date.

### Round 308. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Sub-state licensing: home improvement contractor and salesperson licences required by New York City and by suburban counties where the state has none, so a lead-gen mode keyed by state misses them
- Attack: New York has no statewide home-improvement salesperson registration, so the profile puts NY in full-close mode. The agent quotes and closes a homeowner in Suffolk County, then one in Queens. The Suffolk County Consumer Affairs home-improvement licence, the NYC DCWP Home Improvement Contractor licence and the NYC home-improvement salesperson licence all require a licensed person to solicit and sign. The policy table has no county or city rows. The design switches to "lead-gen mode in salesperson-registration states," which is state granularity. Licensing that lives at the city and county level goes unmodeled. Under NYC rules and county licensing, an unlicensed sale can make the contract unenforceable and void the seller's right to collect.
- Defense: Contractor licensing is already keyed to the property, not the state. R178 adds "installer_license rows per jurisdiction" with a "model-checked invariant: no QUOTE, READ_BACK, CONTRACT_SENT or install effect unless the installer's licence row is valid for the property's jurisdiction", and a stale feed "fails closed". With no NYC DCWP or Suffolk row, there's no quote. R47's lead-gen mode already exists, so the "contract is issued by the registered salesperson of record". The real gap is that R47 assigns salesperson licensing at state granularity.
- Verdict: R178's installer_license invariant is real, but its rows are state boards (CSLB, ROC, DBPR). For New York, which has no state board, nothing forces a NYC or Suffolk row to exist. R47's lead-gen switch, which governs who may solicit and sign, is explicitly state-granular. R181's sub-state layer covers only tribal land. The NYC home-improvement salesperson licence therefore goes unmodeled, and the defense relies on a NEW layer stack.
- Fix: Make the regulation profile a layer stack (state, county, municipality, tribal) resolved from the parcel geocode, with contractor and salesperson rows allowed on any layer. A locality whose licensing coverage isn't attested defaults to lead-gen. Add the invariant that no on-call price is given unless every layer's salesperson row permits it.

### Round 309. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Address-level tax jurisdiction: the "total cost" read back depends on home-rule city and county sales-tax options and on ZIP codes that straddle city lines
- Attack: A Colorado lead's ZIP spans two municipalities. The calculator prices statewide: state sales tax exempts renewable-energy components. The house is actually in a home-rule city that has not adopted the local exemption, so the city charges its own sales or use tax on the equipment. READ_BACK states a "total cost" with zero tax. The installer later invoices the tax, or the contract total is wrong. Colorado exempts solar components from state tax, but local and home-rule jurisdictions decide for themselves whether to exempt them. The correct total needs a rooftop-level tax jurisdiction, not the state or ZIP. A materially wrong total read at the COMMIT gate defeats the read-back. Sources: tax.colorado.gov sales-use-tax-topics-renewable-energy-components; EnergyBot "Local Option" exemption.
- Defense: A wrong total is already blocked from becoming the customer's loss. R232 says "The company honors that price until expiry", and R154 binds the contract amount to the read-back amount ("the e-sign contract amount and the lender amount must equal" it). An invoiced tax above the read-back total would therefore be an unconsented price increase, which R159 names as Tier A harm, with a "no-penalty exit". R29 has "insufficient-inputs -> no figure". The honest gap is the tax input itself. The calculator has no address-level tax jurisdiction.
- Verdict: R154 binds the e-sign contract amount to the read-back amount, and R232 makes the company honor that price. R159 treats any later increase as a re-disclosed change order with a no-penalty exit, and an unconsented increase as Tier A harm. A missed local use tax therefore costs the seller, not the customer, and the read-back total stays the amount the customer actually owes. In Colorado, contractor use tax on materials is usually the contractor's own cost anyway.

### Round 310. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Municipal utilities and electric co-ops are exempt from state net-metering statutes, and their service territories don't follow city, ZIP or county lines
- Attack: A lead's mailing city is served by the investor-owned utility, so the calculator applies the state's statutory net-metering tariff. The parcel actually sits in a rural electric co-op's territory. The co-op's board sets its own buy-all/sell-all export rate and a solar fixed charge, both outside the state NEM law. Savings are quoted at retail-rate netting the customer will never get. In many states the net-metering mandates bind only regulated investor-owned utilities, while munis and co-ops set rules locally and change them by board vote. The utility serving a house is determined by service-territory maps at the parcel level, which the design never consults. The certified savings fragment is wrong at the source.
- Defense: The utility is never inferred from the mailing city. R156 makes the "utility data API or [bill] extractor" the only writer for verified_usage, and authorization comes from the actual utility account holder. A co-op member authorizes the co-op's own data. R227 keys "calculator rows by (delivery utility, generation provider, schedule)", and "an unknown provider or missing row means no figure". R226 requires an "interconnection_tariff row per utility and program". R228's "bill-reconciliation gate" must "reproduce the customer's actual billed amounts ... or no figure", and an IOU tariff can't reproduce a co-op bill. R212 reads tariff_class from the same API.
- Verdict: The design never infers the utility from the mailing city. R156 makes the utility API or bill extractor the only writer for verified_usage, from the account holder's actual utility. R227 keys calculator rows by delivery utility with "missing row means no figure", and the R228 bill-reconciliation gate would fail if an IOU tariff were applied to co-op bills. All three are in the design as cited.

### Round 311. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Snowbird seasonal vacancy: a part-year home produces most when it is empty, and annual true-up or avoided-cost cash-outs erase the credits the savings figure assumes
- Attack: A Michigan couple's Florida house is occupied November to April. Verified usage shows low summer bills because the house sits empty with the AC off. The system is sized to annual kWh and pitched as "covers your bill." From May to October, peak production exports into a vacant house, credits pile up, and the annual true-up pays the excess at avoided cost, a fraction of retail. The real bill savings are a fraction of what was quoted. QUALIFY asks "move horizon" but not occupancy pattern. Verified usage averages over vacancy, and the calculator nets annually at retail. For seasonal residents, timing mismatch and true-up rules make the savings claim unsubstantiated.
- Defense: Annual averaging isn't how savings are computed. R157 requires "the customer's exact tariff schedule ... on verified interval data, else no figure". R226 computes the post-solar bill "on verified interval data" under the mandated interconnection tariff. Hourly vacancy and summer export at the export rate therefore show up in the modeled bill. R229 requires "every fixed, minimum-bill ... component" and says "you will still pay about <residual_bill>". R18's banned-claims lint already blocks "zero bill", so "covers your bill" can't be spoken. R228 reconciliation catches a misread tariff. The remaining gap is that the annual true-up cash-out isn't a required tariff field.
- Verdict: The attack assumes annual retail netting, but R157 requires the exact tariff schedule on verified interval data, and R226 models the post-solar bill on that data. Summer vacancy and its exports therefore appear in the model. R18 bans "zero bill", R229 forces a spoken residual bill, and R210 caps sizing to verified usage. Making true-up and cash-out mandatory tariff fields polishes an already-sound "exact tariff" rule.

### Round 312. Winner: Designer
- Topic: Cancellation-window arithmetic: "midnight of the third business day" depends on the buyer's state holiday calendar and on time-zone lines inside states and reservations
- Attack: A buyer in Arizona's Navajo Nation portion (which observes DST, unlike the rest of Arizona) or in Florida's Central-time panhandle signs Friday before a state-only holiday. Ops code computes the window on the federal holiday calendar and the dialer's or the state capital's time zone. It treats a cancel emailed at 11:20pm local on day three as late and schedules install for the "day after the longest window." Business-day counts exclude state-specific holidays, and the cutoff is midnight where the buyer is. Time-zone boundaries split states and counties, so neither the state nor the county gives the zone. A timely rescission gets refused and work starts inside a live cancellation right.
- Defense: The design never runs at the statutory edge. R15 applies the "longest statutory cancellation window (CA 65+ 5 business days) for all", and install "waits out the longest cancellation window". A cancel on day three of a three-day window is inside our five-day window wherever the buyer lives. R216 makes "oral cancellation to the agent valid notice". R194 processes a cancel "at once with no friction ... frozen in the customer's favour". R159 and R141 add a survey and mailed notice inside the window before install. Time zones are taken from pinned tzdata (R139). Holiday arithmetic is the residual risk.
- Verdict: R15 applies the longest window (5 business days) to every buyer, and install waits it out. A cancel on day three of a three-day statutory window is therefore well inside the design's own window, wherever the buyer is and whatever state holiday intervenes. R216 accepts oral cancels, R194 freezes the window in the customer's favour, and R139 pins tzdata.

### Round 313. Winner: Breaker
- Topic: Border towns: rural mailing addresses use a post office across the state line, so the lead record puts the house in the wrong state and nothing in the call ever corrects it
- Attack: A house in Arkansas has a "Texarkana, TX" mailing address, a regional area code and a ZIP whose post office is in Texas. The profile loads Texas rules: contract law, home-solicitation cancellation, licensing and the Texas utility market. The caller confirms "yes, Texarkana" during IDENTITY. jurisdiction_changed never fires because the caller never contradicts the record. Contract, disclosures, utility and AHJ are all wrong-state. The jurisdiction_changed guard reacts only to in-call cues. Here the record and the caller agree and are both wrong. USPS city and state reflect the delivering post office, not the parcel's state, county or AHJ, and only a parcel/geocode lookup at the pre-dial gate would catch it.
- Defense: The design already relies on parcel data, not USPS city. R88 runs a "record-owner lookup on validated address", and R204 and R205 read "county parcel data" and "parcel use code". A Texarkana, TX parcel search finds no fee owner for an Arkansas house, so R181 "fails closed to human review". R156 and R212 identify the serving utility and its state from the utility account API, so the tariff can't be Texas's. R181 checks boundaries on the geocode. R70 adds candidates on disagreement. The gap is that no rule explicitly says the jurisdiction key comes from the parcel.
- Verdict: The parcel and utility reads exist (R88, R204, R205, R156, R212), but no rule makes the parcel the jurisdiction key. R22 validates the postal address, which returns Texarkana, TX. The fail-closed "no fee owner" clause sits in R181's tribal fix and triggers only if the lookup happens to fail. A geocoding vendor would find the Arkansas owner and pass, leaving the Texas profile to drive contract, cancellation and licensing. The defense concedes the gap.
- Fix: A pre-dial resolver geocodes to a rooftop point and then a parcel (state, county, municipality, AHJ, tribal, tax and utility territory). Postal city and state are never the jurisdiction key. If postal and parcel states disagree, both become candidates with most-restrictive-wins, and there is no close until a human confirms the parcel. The utility API's service address must match the parcel state.
